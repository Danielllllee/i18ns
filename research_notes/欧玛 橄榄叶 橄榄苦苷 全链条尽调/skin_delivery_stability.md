## 1. Trade-name olive leaf ingredients sold to cosmetic manufacturers (supplier, INCI, carrier, oleuropein content, use level, claims)

### Takeaway
Cosmetic-positioned olive leaf ingredients come in two very different forms: dry standardized extracts (Deretil NaturOlive OE12S at 12% and OE35S at 35% oleuropein; Sabinsa "Oleuropein 80" at 80%) and dilute liquid extracts in glycerin, propanediol or water (stated oleuropein from a 0.4% minimum up to 7.5%, or not stated at all, e.g. Berkem R0570 and Hallstar Eurol BT). The recommended use levels I found run from 0.1% to 5%; none found recommends 10%.

### Inferences
- A "10% olive leaf extract" label cannot be converted to an oleuropein percentage without knowing which of these forms was used. The ingredients above span 0.4% (a Thai glycerin blend, stated minimum) to 80% oleuropein (Sabinsa), a 200-fold range.
- Every recommended use level found is 5% or lower (Lotioncrafter 2-5%, Thai blend 0.1-5%, Hallstar Eurol BT 0.1-1%, patent at most 0.5%). A 10% loading is therefore above the range suppliers publish, which is consistent with a dilute liquid extract, a very high dose of a dry extract, or a "10%" that counts something else (e.g. the extract fraction of a glycol/water solution as in Berkem's 10/40/50 composition). This is my reading; the brand's COA would settle it.
- No glycerin- or butylene-glycol-carried olive leaf extract with a published oleuropein assay was found apart from the Thai blend's 0.4% minimum. The only liquid with an explicit high assay (7.5%) is carried in propanediol.

- Not found: use levels for Deretil NaturOlive OE12S/OE35S and Sabinsa Oleuropein 80 (hidden or absent); oleuropein or total polyphenol percentage for Berkem R0570 and Hallstar Eurol BT; specifications for Bio-Botanica Olive Leaf In Glycerin and Gerbex Olive; any butylene glycol carrier; independent (non-vendor) test results for any of these ingredients.
- The ingredient actually used in Phyto-C / 欧玛 O-Live Gel was not identified in this task. Chinese-market raw-material suppliers for it are not covered.
- Unverified lead: one search excerpt attributed two statements to a Chinese popular-science article: that olive leaf extract contains about 3%-18% oleuropein, and that a 10% formula loading is far above the raw-material supplier's recommended 1%. The article was probably the 澎湃号 piece on 油橄榄 products at [thepaper.cn newsDetail_forward_15620543](https://www.thepaper.cn/newsDetail_forward_15620543), which the sibling notes also cite, but the excerpt did not say so, and a follow-up search could not confirm either number. Do not quote them before opening the article.

## 2. Dry olive leaf extract grades sold for supplements (20%, 40%, 70% oleuropein): how they are specified

### Takeaway
Dry grades are specified as a minimum oleuropein content by HPLC (6%, 10%, 18%, 20%, 40%; one listing offers 10-50%), with a loss-on-drying limit of 5-8%. A carrier is named only for the Naturex 18% grade (maltodextrin). No 70% grade specification was found in two searches.

### Cited Findings
- Naturex (Givaudan), "Olive Leaf PE 18% Oleuropein HPLC" (EA840420): composition is natural extract plus maltodextrin; oleuropein minimum 18%; loss on drying limit 8%. — [Knowde, Naturex](https://knowde.com/stores/givaudan-naturex/products/naturex-olive-leaf-pe-18-oleuropein-hplc-ea840420)
- Cambridge Commodities, spec 1503: assay minimum 18% oleuropein; loss on drying maximum 5%. A second sheet (spec 15034) exists from the same supplier but was not described in the excerpt. — [Cambridge Commodities spec 1503](https://cambridgecommodities.com/ugc-1/pdfs/spec-1503.pdf); [spec 15034](https://www.cambridgecommodities.com/ugc-1/pdfs/spec-15034.pdf)
- Parchem, powdered olive leaf extract (CAS 84012-27-1): oleuropein by HPLC at least 6.0%; loss on drying at most 5%; heavy metals at most 10 ppm. A separate Parchem listing offers a dry extract of 18-20% oleuropein by HPLC. — [Parchem 6% sheet](https://parchem.com/api/get-pdf/olive-leaf-powdered-extract-6-001794.pdf); [Parchem olive leaf listing](https://parchem.com/chemical-supplier-distributor/olive-leaf-021921)
- Total Ingredients, "Olive Leaf Extract 20% Oleuropein by HPLC": the title says 20%, but the specification sheet sets the oleuropein assay at 10% or more by HPLC (title and spec disagree); loss on drying at most 5.0%; shelf life 36 months. Its 40% sheet gives loss on drying at most 8.0% (assay row cut off in the excerpt). 6% and 10% listings also exist (titles only). — [Total Ingredients 20%](https://total-ingredients.com/products/olive-leaf-extract-20-oleuropein-by-hplc); [40%](https://total-ingredients.com/products/olive-leaf-extract-40-by-hplc); [10%](https://total-ingredients.com/products/olive-leaf-extract-10-oleuropein-by-hplc); [6%](https://total-ingredients.com/products/olive-extract-6-oleuropein-by-hplc)
- SupplySide Global 2025 listing, "Olive Extract": specification oleuropein at least 40% (HPLC) and, separately, hydroxytyrosol at least 40% (HPLC); the supplier states compliance with the Chinese Pharmacopoeia, USP and European Pharmacopoeia (a supplier assertion). — [SupplySide listing](https://attendees.supplysideshow.com/event/supplyside-global-2025/product/UHJvZHVjdF8xMzE4MDQ5)
- HSF Biotech oleuropein powder: grades 10%, 20%, 30% and 40%, tested by HPLC; shelf life two years if stored cool and dry, away from strong light and heat. — [HSF Biotech](https://www.hsfbiotech.com/vegenutri-nutrition-ingredients/oleuropein.html)
- A CPHI Online listing offers olive leaf extract in a 10-50% range with HPLC as the test method (no detailed specification). — [CPHI Online](https://www.cphi-online.com/product/olive-leaf-extract-2/)
- Banner Bio lists 20.0% and 40.0% oleuropein powders (see Question 1). Sabinsa's 80% cosmetic grade and Deretil's 12% and 35% grades are in Question 1.
- 70% grade: two searches found no specification. The highest assays found were 80% (Sabinsa Oleuropein 80) and 40% (SupplySide).
- Manufacturing context from patents: a patent returned by the 70% search describes extraction giving a dry powder of roughly 30-40% oleuropein by weight, with processing kept below about 88 °C because the glucoside is destroyed at higher temperatures. The excerpt did not say which patent it was; candidates are [US 10,258,660](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10258660), [US 5,714,150](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5714150), [US 8,574,635](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8574635) and [US 9,132,145](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9132145). It is not tied to any commercial grade.

### Inferences
- The commodity grades cluster at 6%, 10%, 18-20% and 40% oleuropein. The 18-20% grade looks like the mid-market standard, and the 20% sheet whose spec reads "10% or more" shows that the headline grade on a listing can differ from the guaranteed minimum.
- Specifications are "minimum by HPLC", not typical values. Assay basis (as-is or dried), HPLC reference standard and tolerance were not visible, so real lots can run higher than the minimum.
- Higher grades (40% and above) imply purification steps. Deretil's OE12S is water-only, while Sabinsa's 80% grade lists methanol, chloroform and ethyl acetate, so solvent residue questions differ by grade.

### Gaps
- No 70% oleuropein grade specification found.
- Solvent and carrier were stated only for Naturex 18% (maltodextrin), Deretil OE12S (water only) and Sabinsa 80 (organic solvents, per the Knowde excerpt); the other grades say nothing about either.
- No COA, HPLC method, reference standard or assay basis was available for any grade.

## 3. Calculation: oleuropein in a finished product containing 10% of each ingredient, versus the 0.1% used by Perugini et al. 2008

### Takeaway
At 10% inclusion, dry standardized grades give 0.6% to 8.0% oleuropein (6 to 80 times Perugini's 0.1%), a 7.5%-standardized propanediol liquid gives 0.75% (7.5 times), and a glycerin blend with only a 0.4% minimum gives 0.04% (0.4 times). The label "10% olive leaf extract" alone fixes none of these; the ingredient's own assay decides.

### Cited Findings
- Perugini et al., Int J Cosmet Sci 2008;30:113-120, tested an O/W emulsion and an emulgel containing oleuropein on healthy volunteers, with vitamin E as reference. The abstract reports reductions of about 22% (erythema), 35% (TEWL) and 30% (blood flow). — [Univ. Pavia IRIS abstract](https://iris.unipv.it/handle/11571/140790)
- The string "Oleuropein content: 0.1 % w/w" appears in the full text, but no excerpt states explicitly that both vehicles contained 0.1%. The oleuropein was prepared from whole olive leaves in a Naviglio extractor with distilled water at room temperature and then purified by chromatography; the purity percentage was not retrieved. (Relayed from the sibling note human_clinical_evidence.md, rows A2 and A3.) — [Perugini 2008 full text, olivelife.gr copy](https://www.olivelife.gr/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Efficacy-of-oleuropein-against-UVB-irradiation-preliminary-evaluation.pdf)
- Do not use 288.6 µg/mL (extract) and 255.1 µg/mL (final formulation) as Perugini's concentration. A search for those exact numbers tied them to a 2026 Pharmaceutics paper by Alparslan and colleagues on an oleuropein-enriched oral spray gel (published 3 Feb 2026), not to Perugini 2008. The sibling note raw_material_chemistry.md (line 189) attributes them to Perugini via a Rev Ceres review, which my search did not support. — [PMC12944263](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12944263/); [ISTUN open-access repository record](https://openaccess.istun.edu.tr/entities/publication/87f43797-2d9e-4f46-a25e-31a262f95c11); the Rev Ceres review is a 2020 literature review without a formulation study: [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/b6a99cb91446458d8d872c42977ca46e)
- Input specifications used below are cited in Questions 1 and 2 (Sabinsa Oleuropein 80, Deretil OE12S and OE35S, Voyageur 7.5% propanediol liquid, MySkinRecipes 0.4% minimum blend, Naturex and Cambridge 18%, Parchem 6%, Total Ingredients 10% and 20%, SupplySide and Total Ingredients 40%, Banner 20% and 40%).

### Inferences
**CALCULATION.** Formula: oleuropein in product (% w/w) = inclusion (% w/w) x oleuropein fraction of ingredient (% w/w) / 100. Baseline: Perugini 0.1% w/w = 1 mg per g of product (0.1 g / 100 g). Inclusion = 10%.

| Ingredient (basis) | Oleuropein in ingredient | Arithmetic at 10% inclusion | Oleuropein in product | Multiple of 0.1% | Inclusion needed to reach 0.1% (0.1 / fraction) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Sabinsa Oleuropein 80 | 80% | 10 x 80 / 100 | 8.0% | 80x | 0.125% |
| Dry 40% grade (SupplySide, Total Ingredients, Banner) | 40% | 10 x 40 / 100 | 4.0% | 40x | 0.25% |
| Deretil NaturOlive OE35S | 35% | 10 x 35 / 100 | 3.5% | 35x | 0.29% |
| Dry 20% grade (Banner, Total Ingredients title) | 20% | 10 x 20 / 100 | 2.0% (1.0% if the sheet's "10% or more" is the true assay) | 20x (10x) | 0.50% |
| Dry 18% grade (Naturex, Cambridge) | 18% | 10 x 18 / 100 | 1.8% | 18x | 0.56% |
| Deretil NaturOlive OE12S | 12% | 10 x 12 / 100 | 1.2% | 12x | 0.83% |
| Dry 10% grade (Total Ingredients) | 10% | 10 x 10 / 100 | 1.0% | 10x | 1.0% |
| Dry 6% grade (Parchem) | 6% | 10 x 6 / 100 | 0.6% | 6x | 1.67% |
| Liquid in propanediol (Voyageur) | 7.5% | 10 x 7.5 / 100 | 0.75% | 7.5x | 1.33% |
| Glycerin blend (MySkinRecipes), stated minimum | at least 0.4% | 10 x 0.4 / 100 | at least 0.04% | at least 0.4x | at most 25% |

- For Berkem-type compositions, 10% of the ingredient carries 10 x 10 / 100 = 1.0% "aqueous extract" into the product. Illustration only (hypothetical): if that fraction had the oleuropein content of a dry 6%, 18% or 40% grade, the product would contain 0.06%, 0.18% or 0.40% oleuropein. If the "aqueous extract" is itself a dilute liquid, the values would be lower.
- Spread at 10% inclusion: 0.04% to 8.0% oleuropein, a 200-fold range (8.0 / 0.04). Only the glycerin blend with a 0.4% minimum falls below Perugini's 0.1%; every dry grade (6% to 80%) and the 7.5% liquid exceeds it by 6 to 80 times.
- Solubility check (CALCULATION plus the solubility figures in Question 4): 3 g/L is 0.3% w/v and 9.5 g/L is 0.95% w/v. At 10% inclusion, dry grades of 12% or more would put 1.2% or more oleuropein into the product, above both figures in a plain aqueous gel unless glycols raise solubility. Vendor solubilities as high as 50-92 mg/mL (5-9.2% w/v) exist, so this is uncertain.
- Practical reading: if O-Live Gel's "10% olive leaf extract" is a dry standardized extract of at least 6% oleuropein, the gel would carry at least 0.6% oleuropein, six or more times Perugini's concentration. If it is a typical dilute liquid extract, it could carry less than Perugini's 0.1%. Ask the brand for the ingredient COA (HPLC oleuropein, % w/w, with solvent/carrier).

### Gaps
- The brand's actual raw material, its form (dry or liquid), carrier and oleuropein assay are unknown.
- Perugini's 0.1% w/w is partially verified (text string found, not tied to both vehicles) and the purity of the isolated oleuropein is not known.
- The 70% row is arithmetic on a hypothetical grade, not a sourced specification.
- Assay basis (as-is or dried) for dry grades is unknown, so the multiples above may be slightly off for dry-basis specifications.

## 4. Oleuropein and hydroxytyrosol recovered from skin layers after cream, emulsion or gel; logP and water solubility

### Takeaway
I found no published measurement of oleuropein or hydroxytyrosol recovered, as a percent of dose, from stratum corneum, epidermis and dermis after a conventional cream, emulsion or gel on human or pig skin. The nearest evidence shows low passive permeation: negligible hydroxytyrosol crossed the membrane from a cream, oleogel and hydrogel, authors call hydroxytyrosol's percutaneous absorption "very low", and nanocarrier papers were built because oleuropein penetrates poorly. Only computed logP values (-0.45 to +0.63) were found, and reported water solubility ranges from 0.25 mg/mL to 92 mg/mL.

### Cited Findings
**Skin-layer or skin-permeation data (none is oleuropein in a conventional cream, emulsion or gel on human or pig skin)**
- El-Gogary et al., Expert Opin Drug Deliv 2021;18(10):1523-1532: an oleuropein microemulsion (particle size 30.25 +/- 4.8 nm, stable one year at room temperature) was developed "to overcome the poor skin penetration of oleuropein"; the selected formulation showed total deposition in skin layers of 95.67%. An MDPI review in the same result set (exact paper not confirmed; candidate: Pharmaceuticals 2025;18(4):573) describes the ex vivo model as albino rat dorsal skin, while an ACS Omega review refers to deposition in the psoriatic skin of patients, so the model should be checked against the original paper. The breakdown by stratum corneum, epidermis and dermis, the applied dose and any comparator vehicle were not in the excerpts. — [Taylor & Francis abstract](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/17425247.2021.1932813); [agya.info record](https://agya.info/publications/books-articles/oleuropein-as-a-novel-topical-antipsoriatic-nutraceutical-formulation-in-microemulsion-nanocarrier-and-exploratory-clinical-appraisal); [MDPI review candidate](https://www.mdpi.com/1424-8247/18/4/573); [ACS Omega review](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/acsomega.2c08016)
- Al Saqr et al., Pharm Dev Technol 2022, hydroxytyrosol niosome gel on human cadaver skin: the authors state "due to its hydrophilic nature, percutaneous absorption and transdermal delivery of HT are very low". The niosome gel raised hydroxytyrosol flux 28-fold over aqueous solution and 4.4-fold over ethanolic solution (both p < 0.001); lecithin-containing formulations lowered flux and gave two-fold higher skin retention than the solutions (p < 0.05). Absolute percent of dose per layer was not in the excerpt. — [Ajman University record](https://research.ajman.ac.ae/en/publications/niosomal-formulation-of-hydroxytyrosol-a-polyphenolic-antioxidant/)
- Cometa et al., Molecules 2022;27(13):4273: a hydroxytyrosol-rich olive fruit extract in hydrogel, oleogel and cream, tested in Franz cells. Permeated polyphenols were 272.1 +/- 1.8 GAE/g (hydrogel), 174 +/- 10 (oleogel) and 179.6 +/- 2 (cream); only a negligible amount of hydroxytyrosol itself crossed the membrane from all three. These are Folin total-polyphenol units, and the membrane type (skin or synthetic) was not retrieved. — [PMC9268399](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9268399/)
- Alonso et al., J Pharm Pharmacol 2015: hydroxytyrosol and its hexanoate and octanoate esters (and gallic acid esters) on porcine skin biopsies in Franz static cells; antioxidant residues were found in every skin layer, and the more hydrophobic esters were absorbed in higher amounts. No numbers were retrieved. — [Sigma-Aldrich abstract listing](https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/AT/de/tech-docs/paper/635887)
- Smeriglio et al., DARU J Pharm Sci 2019;27(1):283-293: a commercial hydroxytyrosol-based cream (Fenolia Eudermal Cream 15) on a reconstructed human epidermis model; hydroxytyrosol passage was measured by RP-LC-DAD and the abstract says "excellent transcutaneous absorption values were found for HT". The numbers were not retrieved, and a reconstructed epidermis is not human or pig skin. — [Springer article](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s40199-019-00274-3)
- Allaw et al., Nanomedicine 2021;16(26):2363-2376: oleuropein in collagen-enriched transfersomes, glycerosomes and glytransfersomes for skin delivery; about 87% entrapment; vesicles stable over four months; the excerpt covered fibroblast results only. Sklenarova et al., Eur J Pharm Biopharm 2023;185:126-136 co-loaded oleuropein and lentisk oil in phospholipid vesicles; skin deposition figures were not in the excerpt. — [Allaw 2021 record](https://iris.unica.it/handle/11584/323090); [Sklenarova 2023 PDF](https://iris.unica.it/bitstream/11584/374743/3/1-s2.0-S0939641123000607-main.pdf)
- Int J Food Sci Nutr 2022;73(4) is titled "Dietary polyphenol oleuropein and its metabolite hydroxytyrosol are moderate skin permeable elastase and collagenase inhibitors..."; the permeability data behind "moderate skin permeable" were not visible. — [Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/09637486.2021.1996542)

### Inferences
- The consumer-report question "does a conventional leave-on gel put meaningful oleuropein into skin?" has no direct measurement behind either answer. Absence of a published layer-by-layer recovery for a plain cream or gel is itself a finding.
- The indirect evidence points the same way: oleuropein (MW 540.5, TPSA about 202 Å², six hydrogen-bond donors, logP near zero by calculation) and hydroxytyrosol are described by the authors of several formulation studies as poorly penetrating, and negligible hydroxytyrosol crossed a membrane from a cream, oleogel and hydrogel. The molecular-size rule of thumb for passive skin penetration (about 500 Da) is general knowledge that I did not source in this session; cite or omit.
- The 95.67% "total deposition in skin layers" for the microemulsion shows that an engineered nanocarrier can load rat skin, but the denominator (percent of dose or of what was recovered), the layer split and the comparator are unknown. It does not transfer to a plain gel.
- Perugini's soothing effects at 0.1% oleuropein show some activity on UVB-damaged (barrier-disrupted) skin; they do not show how much oleuropein reached viable layers.
- Solubility reports span about 370-fold (0.25 to 92 mg/mL), so no single number should be quoted without its conditions (water vs PBS, temperature, sonication). The computed logP values disagree in sign (-0.45 to +0.63), which is another reason not to quote one value as "the" logP.

## 5. Oleuropein shelf stability in creams and gels beyond Perugini 2008, and what pH or packaging conditions improved it

### Takeaway
Perugini's Table II (O/W emulsion 82.68 versus emulsion gel 54.25, read as percent of initial oleuropein, after 1 month at 37 °C) is the only direct retention measurement in cosmetic vehicles that I found, and it was relayed from a sibling researcher rather than re-retrieved by me. Other evidence comes from aqueous leaf extracts (first-order loss, half-life 13.1 days at 24 °C, strongly pH- and enzyme-dependent) and from pharmaceutical semi-solids whose retention numbers were not retrievable (Ghodrati 2026 favored a cream at pH about 5.4). No packaging-specific data on oleuropein were found.

### Cited Findings
- Perugini 2008 Table II (relayed from sibling note human_clinical_evidence.md, row A16, not re-retrieved): O/W emulsion 92 +/- 19.87 (1 month, 20 °C), 82.68 +/- 17.11 (1 month, 37 °C), 93 +/- 10.09 (freeze-thaw); emulsion gel 104 +/- 2.65 (20 °C), 54.25 +/- 1.35 (37 °C), 105 +/- 9.94 (freeze-thaw). The authors report strong variation in oleuropein content at 37 °C and conclude low thermal stability in both formulations. The unit and basis (presumably percent of initial oleuropein), the formulation pH and the packaging were not retrieved. — [Perugini 2008 full text, olivelife.gr copy](https://www.olivelife.gr/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Efficacy-of-oleuropein-against-UVB-irradiation-preliminary-evaluation.pdf)
- Perugini 2008 process detail (relayed from sibling note human_studies_details_round2.md, row A11; a paraphrase, moderate confidence): oleuropein was added during the cooling phase of production, at about 40 °C. Full formulas, the HPLC method, pH and packaging were not found in any excerpt. — [Perugini 2008 full text, olivelife.gr copy](https://www.olivelife.gr/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Efficacy-of-oleuropein-against-UVB-irradiation-preliminary-evaluation.pdf)
- Aqueous leaf extracts, first-order loss of oleuropein: half-lives of about 3.4 days at 80 °C, 9.0 days at 60 °C, 10.2 days at 40 °C and 13.1 days at room temperature (24 °C); about 6.2 days at 14 °C and 9.2 days at 7 °C (the authors note a change in behavior at low temperature). — [Antioxidants 2021;10(12):1963, PMC8750354](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8750354/); [table 2](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8750354/table/antioxidants-10-01963-t002)
- pH: an isothermal study (5-90 °C, pH 2.0-6.0) reported first-order degradation, higher degradation at pH 2.0, an activation energy of 98.03 +/- 0.08 kJ/mol, and about 100-fold slower degradation at higher pH. The excerpt does not name the paper; the RSC article below is the likeliest match among the results. — [RSC Sustainable Food Technology, d4fb00044g](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/fb/d4fb00044g)
- Conflicting pH and enzyme evidence: a Chinese study found oleuropein degrades more easily in alkaline than acidic surroundings, linked much of the loss in extracts to beta-glucosidase, and reported that after the enzyme was inactivated by boiling the oleuropein was not decomposed in 30 days. — [Chinese study (SPKJ201304024)](https://www.spgykj.com/cn/article/id/SPKJ201304024)
- Light: UV-C irradiation broke oleuropein in ethanol or water down into hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid; without UV-C the compound was fairly stable in those solvents. Only UV-C was tested. — [Univ. Bozen-Bolzano record](https://bia.unibz.it/esploro/outputs/journalArticle/Effect-of-light-irradiation-on-the/991005772981501241)
- Stabilization by formulation: for an ocular product, cyclodextrin complexation followed by liposome encapsulation greatly increased oleuropein stability in aqueous solution, especially at 4 °C and for extruded liposomes. — [PMC8619189](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8619189/)
- Ghodrati et al., Pharm Dev Technol 2026;31(4):642-664 (DOI 10.1080/10837450.2026.2666098): oleuropein isolated from olive leaves at 1%, 2.5% and 5% in an ointment (F1), cream (F2) and gel (F3); content and viscosity were monitored under short-term, long-term and accelerated conditions; the cream F2.1 gave the best profile, with a skin-compatible pH of about 5.4 and high initial viscosity. The retention numbers were not in the abstract. — [Ankara University record](https://avesis.ankara.edu.tr/yayin/0220f4f9-1641-4e71-8ffb-0768610e1b98/development-and-enhanced-stability-of-an-oleuropein-loaded-topical-formulation-for-modified-delivery-and-potential-use-for-wound-and-burn-therapy)
- Al-Rimawi et al.: day creams with 0.1%, 0.4% and 1.0% w/w olive leaf extract standardized for oleuropein, compared with a commercial cream, kept a stable, homogeneous appearance and texture over three months at room temperature; oleuropein content over time was not shown in the excerpt. — [Al-Quds repository](https://dspace.alquds.edu/handle/20.500.12213/811)
- Alparslan et al., Pharmaceutics 2026, oleuropein oral spray gel (not a skin product): pH held at 6.90 +/- 0.02 with no relevant viscosity change during storage; oleuropein content over time was not retrieved. — [PMC12944263](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12944263/)
- Other nanocarrier stability statements (not content assays of a cream): microemulsion stable one year at room temperature (El-Gogary 2021) and collagen vesicles stable four months (Allaw 2021); see Question 4 links.
- Vendor storage advice for dry oleuropein powder: cool, dry place, not frozen, away from strong light and heat. — [HSF Biotech](https://www.hsfbiotech.com/vegenutri-nutrition-ingredients/oleuropein.html)

### Inferences
- CALCULATION from Perugini Table II: after 1 month at 37 °C the emulsion lost 100 - 82.68 = 17.32 percentage points and the gel lost 100 - 54.25 = 45.75. The emulsion's 37 °C value overlaps its own 20 °C value within the reported spread (82.68 +/- 17.11 versus 92 +/- 19.87), while the gel's loss is clear (54.25 +/- 1.35 versus 104 +/- 2.65). At 20 °C both vehicles kept about 92-104% for one month. If O-Live Gel is a water-based gel, the Perugini emulgel result is the closest direct analogue and indicates large potency loss at warm temperatures. The vehicle type of O-Live Gel was not established here, and an emulgel is not necessarily the same as a hydrogel, so this is an inference about vehicle type, not proof for any other gel.
- CALCULATION from leaf-extract kinetics (first order, fraction left = 0.5 ^ (days / half-life)): at 24 °C and a half-life of 13.1 days, 0.5 ^ (30/13.1) = 20% remains after 30 days and 0.9% after 90 days; at 40 °C and 10.2 days, 13% remains after 30 days. These apply to crude aqueous leaf extracts, not to a finished gel. Perugini's much better retention of purified oleuropein in an emulsion (82.68% at 37 °C) suggests that crude, enzyme-containing extracts degrade faster than purified oleuropein in a formulated vehicle; the Chinese study's finding that inactivating beta-glucosidase stopped loss over 30 days fits that explanation. This is an inference.
- Evidence on pH is mixed (faster loss at pH 2 in one study, faster loss in alkali in another), so no optimum pH for oleuropein in a cosmetic gel is established in what I found. Ghodrati's choice of a cream at pH about 5.4 is a formulation preference, not a proven stability optimum.
- No packaging effect (opaque, airless, oxygen-barrier) has been measured for oleuropein in a cosmetic that I could find. Light sensitivity is documented only for UV-C. Cold storage (4 °C), enzyme inactivation and encapsulation (cyclodextrin plus liposomes) are the conditions shown to help, none in a conventional gel.

### Gaps
- Not found: oleuropein retention numbers for any cosmetic cream or gel other than Perugini 2008 (Ghodrati 2026 and Al-Rimawi retention data are behind the abstracts); pH or packaging conditions that improved retention in a cosmetic product; the pH and packaging used in Perugini's formulations.
- Not tied to a source I could open: a statement in a search excerpt that a separate Palestinian olive-leaf study found oleuropein contents fell significantly with higher temperature and longer storage and suggested a one-year expiry (candidate records at dspace.alquds.edu, for example [handle 20.500.12213/59](https://dspace.alquds.edu/handle/20.500.12213/59)); a Molecules 2020 table ([PMC7024224](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7024224/table/molecules-25-00420-t004)) tracking oleuropein plus derivatives at 0.5, 1 and 6 months whose product matrix was not identified.
- The Perugini Table II values are relayed, and their unit and basis were not visible to the sibling researcher either.
