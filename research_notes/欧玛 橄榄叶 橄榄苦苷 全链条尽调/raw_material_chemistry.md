## Q1. What "Olea Europaea Leaf Extract" contains: oleuropein's identity and physical chemistry, and the other major leaf constituents

### Takeaway
Oleuropein is a secoiridoid glycoside: C25H32O13, 540.5 g/mol, PubChem CID 5281544, CAS 32619-42-4. Enzymes or hydrolysis split it into glucose plus an aglycone, and the aglycone then splits into hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid. Its measured water solubility is about 9.5 g/L. LogP, pKa and UV maxima/ε were **not** retrieved; HPLC methods detect it at 230, 238 or 280 nm. A leaf extract is a mixture. Besides oleuropein it contains verbascoside, luteolin and apigenin glycosides, rutin and hydroxytyrosol. Leaves also hold a lot of triterpenes (oleanolic acid about 1.7–3.5% of dry weight), which partition by solvent. Even an unpurified 70% ethanol dry extract was about 74% non-oleuropein matter.

### Cited Findings
**Identity and physical chemistry**
- Formula C25H32O13, molecular weight 540.5 g/mol, CID 5281544. PubChem describes it as a "secoiridoid glycoside" — [PubChem CID 5281544](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/5281544). NCATS gives MW 540.5138 — [NCATS GSRS](https://drugs.ncats.io/substance/2O4553545L).
- The CAS number 32619-42-4 appears in the PubChem synonym list — [CAPS mirror of CID 5281544](https://caps.ncbs.res.in/GRAYU/phytochemical/cid/5281544); [LKT Labs "Oleuropein (CAS 32619-42-4)"](https://lktlabs.com/product/oleuropein/).
- A commercial analytical reference standard is sold at ≥98.0% purity with the same formula and MW (Sigma-Aldrich product 12247) — [Sigma-Aldrich](https://b2b.sigmaaldrich.com/US/en/product/sigma/12247).
- Water solubility measured in a peer-reviewed HPLC-DAD validation paper: **9.5 g/L** (≈9.5 mg/mL) — [PubMed 32839982, "Development and validation of an HPLC-DAD method for determination of oleuropein and other bioactive compounds in olive leaf by-products"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32839982/).
- Vendor solubility figures conflict with that value and with each other: Selleck 50 mg/mL in water at 25 °C — [Selleck](https://www.selleckchem.com/products/oleuropein.html); TargetMol 92 mg/mL with sonication — [TargetMol](https://www.targetmol.com/compound/oleuropein); Cayman ≈0.25 mg/mL in PBS pH 7.2 — [Cayman insert 21220](https://cdn.caymanchem.com/cdn/insert/21220.pdf).
- Structural logic: β-glucosidase first cleaves the glucose, then an esterase hydrolyses the aglycone into hydroxytyrosol + elenolic acid. This confirms the "hydroxytyrosol ester of elenolic-acid glucoside" description. The statement was surfaced from olive-enzyme literature (attribution unverified between these results) — [Velázquez-Palmero et al., Front Plant Sci 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01902/full); [Food Chem 2016, "Oleuropein hydrolysis in natural green olives: importance of the endogenous enzymes"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814616304332).
- Reactive behaviour: when leaf tissue is damaged, foliar β-glucosidase and polyphenol oxidase activate oleuropein into a "very strong protein denaturant" that cross-links proteins and lowers lysine. Konno et al. describe it as a multivalent alkylator — [Konno et al. 1999 PNAS 96:9159](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.96.16.9159).
- The endogenous β-glucosidase can also form the decarboxymethyl dialdehydic aglycone (HyEDA/oleacein) during oil making — [Front Chem 2018 review](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fchem.2018.00239/pdf).
- Detection wavelengths in use: HPLC-PDA at **238 nm** in the 2026 sebocyte paper — [CIMB 2026;48(6):549](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13298046/); DAD at **280 and 230 nm** in a patent LC-MS method — [US 8,574,635 (USPTO PDF)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8574635).

**Other leaf constituents (ranges, with basis)**
- The US 6,743,449 B2 patent (Pinnell & Omar) lists the main components of its extract as oleuropein, hydroxytyrosol, verbascoside, the "triterpenoid" β-sitosterol and 3 flavonoid glycosides — [Google Patents US6743449B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6743449B2/en).
- Dry-leaf basis, ultrasound-assisted extraction: oleuropein 13.386, verbascoside 0.363 and luteolin-4′-O-glucoside 0.527 mg/g of dry powdered leaf — [PMC6165173 (UAE/RSM study)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6165173/).
- Dry-leaf basis, cv. Cobrançosa: verbascoside 966.1 ± 18.1 mg/kg, luteolin-7-O-glucoside 4208.9 ± 97.8 mg/kg and luteolin-4′-O-glucoside 1355.9 ± 75.9 mg/kg [≈1.0, 4.2 and 1.4 mg/g] — [PMC6149345](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6149345/).
- Extract basis, nine genotypes: verbascoside 0.45–21.07 mg/g extract, hydroxytyrosol 1.33–4.03 mg/g extract, luteolin glucosides 1.58–8.67 mg/g extract — [Molecules 2019;24(6):1130, PMC6471253](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6471253/).
- Extract basis, ohmic-heating extraction (maxima): luteolin-7-O-glucoside 4.12, apigenin-7-O-glucoside 3.47 and rutin 3.78 mg/g extract — [Sustainable Food Technol 2024, D3FB00252G](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/fb/d3fb00252g).
- Much lower luteolin-7-O-glucoside (127.36 ± 4.61 and 141.64 ± 0.45 mg/100 g DW) was reported for lyophilised leaves of two Turkish cultivars (attribution unverified; likely this paper) — [PMC13184032 (Gemlik / Kilis Yağlık)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13184032/).
- Triterpenes, by the same Spanish table-olive cultivars: oleanolic acid 17.47, 17.26 and 16.77 g/kg (Hojiblanca, Gordal, Manzanilla); maslinic acid 2.51 (Aloreña) to 3.73 g/kg (Empeltre). The basis is "RM", which the extract did not define — [Foods 2022;11(23):3879](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/11/23/3879).
- Oleanolic acid about 25–35 mg/g DW in leaves — [Oleanolic-acid review, PMC12108409](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12108409/). Other sources give about 3.0–3.5% DW oleanolic and 0.50–0.75% DW maslinic (attribution unverified) — [ScienceDirect Topics: maslinic acid](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/pharmacology-toxicology-and-pharmaceutical-science/maslinic-acid); [Guinda et al., oleanolic acid from olive leaf](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229784073_Supplementation_of_Olis_with_oleanolic_acid_from_the_olive_leaf_Olea_europea_L).
- Oleuropein share of dry extract, 2026 study: 18.40 ± 0.35% (OLW), 21.25 ± 0.14% (OLE50) and 25.73 ± 0.14% w/w (OLE70) — [CIMB 2026;48(6):549](https://doi.org/10.3390/cimb48060549).

### Inferences
- "Olea Europaea Leaf Extract" is not a single substance. Even the most oleuropein-rich unpurified hydroalcoholic dry extract above was about 74% other matter (flavonoid glycosides, verbascoside, hydroxytyrosol, sugars, salts, chlorophyll, and triterpenes depending on solvent).
- Oleuropein is a polar glycoside (about 9.5 g/L in water) while oleanolic and maslinic acids are lipophilic. Solvent polarity therefore sets the oleuropein-to-triterpene ratio: water and low-ethanol extracts favour oleuropein and flavonoid glycosides; high-ethanol or non-polar fractions favour triterpenes and chlorophyll. This is a polarity argument; the retrieved sources did not measure triterpene partitioning by solvent.
- The hydroxytyrosol half of the molecule carries an o-diphenol (catechol). That group is the PPO substrate and drives oxidation and quinone chemistry. Together with the aglycone's dialdehyde formation, it explains the protein reactivity and oxidative browning risks (structural reasoning).

### Gaps
- Experimental logP/logD, pKa and UV λmax/ε for oleuropein were not retrieved; the extracts said only that PubChem lists computed properties. Do not quote values from memory.
- Ranges for ligstroside, oleuroside, tyrosol and β-sitosterol in leaves were not retrieved.
- A "1–14% oleuropein in leaves" range surfaced in a search summary, but the source review could not be identified.
- The 25.73% maximum assumes the CIMB extracts were measured on a dry-extract basis, as stated; extract yields (g extract per g leaf) were not retrieved.

## Q2. Oleuropein content of leaves by cultivar, geography, leaf age and season, and whether an October harvest gives the most

### Takeaway
Reported oleuropein spans from a few tenths of a mg/g to more than 100 mg/g DW. Cultivar, leaf age, drying, extraction and reporting basis drive that range far more than calendar month. Within studies, seasonal effects are real but cultivar-specific. Most multi-month Mediterranean datasets (Spain, Croatia, Arbequina) peak in **late winter to early spring (February–March, the pruning season)**. Datasets from China, Egypt and Nizip in Turkey also favour spring or winter. **October or autumn maxima** appear only in a Turkish Ayvalık dataset and as an August–October plateau in Spanish table-olive cultivars. Young leaves can contain about 10× or more oleuropein than mature leaves.

### Cited Findings
**Season: studies reporting late-winter or spring peaks**
- Spain, *Agronomy* 2021: oleuropein peaked in **March** for Picual (107.17 mg/g) and Manzanilla (102.49 mg/g) [≈10.7% and 10.2% DW], and in **February** for Cornicabra (71.95 mg/g). The authors concluded "the pruning season was the best time" — [Agronomy 2021;11(12):2447](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy11122447).
- Spain, Arbequina: "the highest levels of oleuropein were found around the pruning season, regardless of crop management and plantation framework" — [Horticulturae 2022;8(6):530](https://doi.org/10.3390/horticulturae8060530).
- Croatia, Pasković et al. 2020: sampled October 2017, January 2018 and March 2018. Low air temperatures increased oleuropein in Leccino, Oblica, Levantinka and Drobnica, while Istarska bjelica kept "steady high" levels. The maximum was reported in **March**, averaging 5061.22 mg/100 g DW in Istarska bjelica [≈50.6 mg/g] — [Plants 2020;9(9):1099](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/9/9/1099); [PMC7570285](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7570285/); [PubMed 32867040](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32867040/).
- Croatia: oleuropein was higher in the pruning collection period (CP2) than in the harvest period (CP1) — [Plants 2020;9(12):1667, PMC7760844](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7760844/). A Croatian study also reported that every cultivar had its highest biophenols in March, at pruning (attribution unverified between this paper and [Horticulturae 2023;9(5):594](https://doi.org/10.3390/horticulturae9050594)).
- Croatia 2025 (cultivar × time; sampled October, January, March): most cultivars had their highest DPPH, oleuropein and hydroxytyrosol in March (ST III). "The magnitude, and in eleven cases the direction, of the seasonal change was … cultivar-specific." The paper's statement that autumn is optimal is cited from earlier work, not its own data — [Plants 2025;14(17):2789, PMC12430329](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12430329/).
- Croatia, Central Dalmatia: one search extract said "the highest concentrations of oleuropein were recorded during the low temperatures of March, whereas the lowest … during the warmer period in **October**." A second search could not confirm this, so attribution is unverified — [Central Dalmatia leaves, MDPI 2673-7655/6/5/86](https://www.mdpi.com/2673-7655/6/5/86).
- China, Liangshan: oleuropein and the major flavonoids rose in spring and winter and fell in summer and **autumn**; TPC, TFC and free amino acids were highest in May and December — [Molecules 2019, PMC6963812](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6963812/).
- Egypt, 12 cultivars grown in Egypt: oleuropein, flavonoids and polyphenols were higher in **spring** than autumn. Top values were Manzanillo 218.94, Coratina 151.58 and Agizi Okasi 122.18 mg/100 g of dried leaf *extract* [≈0.22, 0.15 and 0.12% of extract] — [PubMed 33619785](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33619785/). A companion metabolomics paper compared fruiting and flowering seasons — [Sci Rep 2023, PMC9837098](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9837098/).
- Turkey, Nizip Yağlık: the highest oleuropein, **56.738 mg/g**, was in a winter sample from the Kocatepe location (W3). Winter and spring harvests and higher altitudes gave higher antioxidant and enzyme-inhibition potential — [Food Biosci 2024, S2212429224019552](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212429224019552).
- A cultivar × season study: highest in 'Koroneiki' and 'Zard' spring leaves, lowest in 'Mission' **autumn** leaves — [J Herbmed Pharmacol, jhp-45000](https://herbmedpharmacol.com/Article/jhp-45000).
- Italy, Ranalli et al. 2006 (seven cultivars, e.g. Dritta, Leccino, Caroleo, Coratina): genetic factors and leaf colour/age "markedly modified" leaf oleuropein, and some cultivars had more in leaves than in fruit — [J Agric Food Chem 2006;54(2):434–440, PubMed 16417301](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16417301/). A later review cites this paper for "consistently higher in spring than summer" (attribution of the review unverified). Ranalli also published on oleuropein through growth and maturation — [Eur J Lipid Sci Technol 2009](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejlt.200800268).

**Season: studies reporting autumn or October highs**
- Turkey, Ayvalık: "The highest level of oleuropein was found in **autumn** leaf samples, and the levels were significantly lower in samples harvested in the summer." The autumn figure was about 271.66 ppm, versus about 134 ppm in summer — [PMC5430204](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5430204/).
- Spain, table-olive cultivars: April was the lowest month. "In August, the concentration of oleuropein increased and remained constant until **October**, before harvesting the fruit," then fell slightly (not significantly) in Manzanilla and Gordal. December and April were the low points — [Foods 2022;11(23):3879](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/11/23/3879).
- Turkey, Gemlik: 205.20–370.00 mg/kg (2018) and 270.1–369.8 mg/kg (2019) [≈0.2–0.37 mg/g]. Kilis leaves "showed high oleuropein … at late harvest", while elsewhere early harvests gave the most phenolics. Attribution between these two papers is unverified — [DergiPark 2194797](https://dergipark.org.tr/en/download/article-file/2194797); [J Food Compos Anal 2025, S0889157525013377](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0889157525013377).

**Geography and cultivar (Middle East and North Africa)**
- Palestine (West Bank), leaves collected mid-November: dried at room temperature 10.0 mg/g, oven-dried at 50 °C 1.7 mg/g, dried on the tree 2.5 mg/g — [Afaneh et al. 2015, Am J Anal Chem 6:246, doi 10.4236/ajac.2015.63023](https://doi.org/10.4236/ajac.2015.63023); [SCIRP full text](https://www.scirp.org/html/7-2201121_54002.htm).
- Tunisia, cv. Chemlali from nine regions: HPLC-ESI-TOF/IT-MS fingerprints varied significantly by region — [PMC5872618](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5872618/). Tunisian and Algerian varieties: "geographic origin, and variety affect significantly the phenolic content" — [Heliyon 2023, S2405844023094252](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2405844023094252).
- Syria: a study titled "Determination of oleuropein in leaves and fruits of some Syrian olive varieties" exists, but its values were not retrieved — [ResearchGate 281369125](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281369125_Determination_of_oleuropein_in_leaves_and_fruits_of_some_Syrian_olive_varieties).

**Leaf age**
- Young leaves contained oleuropein "an order of magnitude higher (2661–5105 mg/100 g DW) than mature leaves (14.5–302 mg/100 g DW)" [≈26.6–51.1 vs 0.15–3.0 mg/g]. The young leaves formed after a foliar micronutrient treatment, so the comparison is partly confounded — [Plants 2026, doi 10.3390/plants15162503](https://doi.org/10.3390/plants15162503).
- Kilis Yağlık: more phenolics and oleuropein in *young trees*, more flavonoids in old trees (tree age, not leaf age) — [AJTCAM](https://journals.athmsi.org/index.php/ajtcam/article/view/3397).
- Leaf colour/age "markedly modified" oleuropein — [Ranalli et al. 2006](https://pubs.acs.org/doi/10.1021/jf051647b).

### Inferences
- Across these studies the absolute numbers differ by more than 100-fold: about 0.2–0.4 mg/g in the Gemlik and Ayvalık data, about 10 mg/g in Palestine, 50–57 mg/g in Croatia and Nizip, and 72–107 mg/g in Spain. Basis (leaf vs extract, dry vs fresh), drying, extraction and calibration therefore dominate any single "best month" statement. A supplier's harvest month says little unless drying and analysis are specified.
- On balance the multi-timepoint data place the oleuropein maximum in **February–March** in most Mediterranean cultivars studied. Autumn and October maxima are cultivar- or site-specific: Ayvalık, and the Spanish table olives' August–October plateau.
- October coincides with the start of fruit harvest and pruning in many Mediterranean regions, which makes leaf by-product cheap and plentiful then. This is general agronomic context, not verified in this session. A rationale of "October = highest oleuropein" may conflate availability with chemistry.
- Young, light-green leaves (spring flush) can carry about 10× the oleuropein of old leaves. Leaf-age selection could outweigh month of harvest.

### Gaps
- No monthly leaf-oleuropein series was found for Tunisia (Chemlali/Chétoui), Jordan or Syria. No Malik & Bradford seasonal leaf data and no Greek monthly study were retrieved.
- A "4.3–9.2 mg/g in Syrian leaves (n = 7)" figure surfaced in one summary but could not be tied to a source. Treat it as unverified.
- Phyto-C's own statement of the October claim, and its leaf origin, cultivar and harvest documentation, were not found.

## Q3. Enzymology (β-glucosidase, esterase, PPO; the two-component defence) and the effect of drying and enzyme deactivation

### Takeaway
Olive and privet leaves store oleuropein separately from activating enzymes. When tissue is damaged, β-glucosidase (with PPO) converts it into protein-cross-linking aglycone and dialdehyde forms, and esterase action leads on to hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid. Heat (steaming) inactivates the enzymes while oleuropein itself survives. Freeze–thaw and slow, warm, humid drying cause large losses. Results across drying methods conflict and depend on cultivar. Fast high-temperature drying (e.g., 120 °C for 8 min) or cool room-temperature drying often preserves oleuropein best. Intermediate temperatures (about 40–80 °C) with residual moisture are the danger zone.

### Cited Findings
**Two-component defence**
- Konno et al. 1999 (privet, *Ligustrum obtusifolium*): oleuropein is compartmentalised away from activating enzymes in organelles. On tissue damage they "activate oleuropein into a very strong protein denaturant" with protein-cross-linking and lysine-decreasing activity, and proteins treated this way became "innutritive" to insects because lysine was lost — [Konno, Hirayama, Yasui & Nakamura, PNAS 1999;96(16):9159–9164](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.96.16.9159); [PMC17749](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC17749).
- Conference abstracts from the same group: "oleuropein is activated by foliar beta-glucosidase and polyphenol oxidase to show a strong protein-denaturing activity that resembles that of glutaraldehyde." When larvae of *Samia ricini* were fed steamed privet leaves, whose enzymes were inactivated ("oleuropein is heat-stable"), they "grew much faster" (exact abstract attribution unverified) — [ISCE 1999 abstract](https://chemecol.org/programs/1999/o-19.html); [ESA 2001 abstract](https://esa.confex.com/esa/2001/techprogram/paper_3602.htm).
- Privet specialists counter-adapt: 50 mM (0.4%) free glycine was found in the digestive juice of *Brahmaea wallichii* larvae. This is a separate paper, not Konno 1999 — [J Insect Physiol 2001](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022191001001354). A later Konno-group paper states that privet leaves contain about 3% oleuropein per wet weight, citing a 1998 study (attribution unverified) — [ResearchGate 249887885](https://researchgate.net/publication/249887885_Glycine_addition_improves_feeding_performance_of_non-specialist_herbivores_on_the_privet_Ligustrum_obtusifolium_In_vivo_evidence_for_the_physiological_impacts_of_anti-nutritive_plant_defense_with_irid).
- Olive-specific evidence: the defence-related olive β-glucosidase OeGLU, expressed recombinantly in planta, "deglycosylated and activated oleuropein into a strong protein cross-linker." It forms a two-component defence system with developmentally regulated expression — [Koudounas et al. 2015, J Exp Bot 66(7):2093](https://academic.oup.com/jxb/article-pdf/66/7/2093/17138706/erv002.pdf); [UNIC record](https://pure.unic.ac.cy/en/publications/a-defence-related-olea-europaea-%CE%B2-glucosidase-hydrolyses-and-acti/).
- Olive-fruit β-glucosidase (Romero-Segura et al. 2009) shows high specificity for oleuropein. The recombinant fruit enzyme hydrolysed more than 50% of oleuropein within 5 min, and the first products were oleuropein-aglycone isomers — [Front Plant Sci 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01902/full).
- Oleuropein deglucosidated by β-glucosidase gives an aglycone that is the "active form" for collagen cross-linking, a property proposed for leather and biopolymer use — [Antunes et al., Northampton](https://nectar.northampton.ac.uk/id/eprint/3574/); [EP1489135A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP1489135A1/en).
- A paper titled "Evidence of oleuropein degradation by olive leaf protein extract" exists; only the title was retrieved — [Food Chem 2015, S0308814614019189](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814614019189).
- The 2004 patent itself warns that olive actives "are accompanied by certain enzymes capable of affecting hydrolysis of the active fraction," so enzymes must be inactivated, "particularly important when fresh plant materials are used" — [US6743449B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6743449B2/en).

**Drying, freezing and handling: quantitative effects**
- Freeze–thaw: thawing frozen leaf samples for just 5 min cut extractable oleuropein by **57.7%** — [Malik & Bradford, "Recovery and stability of oleuropein and other phenolic compounds during extraction and processing of olive leaves"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43270134_Recovery_and_stability_of_oleuropein_and_other_phenolic_compounds_during_extraction_and_processing_of_olive_Olea_europaea_L_leaves).
- Palestine: room-temperature drying gave 10.0 mg/g, 50 °C oven drying 1.7 mg/g, tree-dried leaves 2.5 mg/g — [Afaneh et al. 2015](https://doi.org/10.4236/ajac.2015.63023).
- Croatia, Leccino vs Istrska belica: for Leccino the ranking was room-temperature air > freeze-drying > 105 °C air; for Istrska belica, freeze-drying > room-temperature air > 105 °C air. The authors concluded freeze-drying "does not seem to be an adequate drying method for improving the extraction of oleuropein" — [Plants 2022;11(7):865, PMC9003305](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9003305/).
- Spain, var. Serrana: hot-air drying at **120 °C for 8 min** gave more phenolics, especially oleuropein, than freeze-drying, and freeze-dried leaves gave extracts with low antioxidant potential — [Ahmad-Qasem et al., "Influence of freezing and dehydration of olive leaves (var. Serrana)…"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257423020_Influence_of_freezing_and_dehydration_of_olive_leaves_var_Serrana_on_extract_composition_and_antioxidant_potential).
- Drying-temperature study: "a specific drying temperature region from 65 to 80 °C" was a declining region for leaf oleuropein — [ResearchGate 269667509](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/269667509_Effect_of_drying_temperature_on_the_oleuropein_content_of_olive_Olea_europea_L_leaves).
- A 2025 process deliberately uses freeze-drying to "promote the selective activation of β-glucosidase and esterase enzymes" and enrich oleacein. That is evidence that freeze-drying damage triggers enzymes once moisture returns — [PMC11764853](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11764853/).
- Solid leaf powder: higher temperature and higher RH accelerate degradation, and low RH stabilises it (kinetics in Q7) — [Antioxidants 2021, PMC8750354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8750354/).
- Process patents specify cool, dark drying and cold alcohol: dry leaves below 35 °C protected from light, then macerate in a low-molecular-weight alcohol below 20 °C — [US "Method for preparing an Olea europaea extract…" (USPTO 6,676,980)](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6676980); [AU2174001A](https://patents.google.com/patent/AU2174001A/en).
- Other drying and storage comparisons exist but their numbers were not retrieved — [Separations 2021;8(9):156](https://doi.org/10.3390/separations8090156); [Tuscan cultivars, J Food Compos Anal 2026](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0889157526004047).

### Inferences
- The data fit an enzyme-plus-moisture model:
  - **Losses** occur when leaves stay moist at temperatures where β-glucosidase and esterase are active (about 40–80 °C, slow drying, delays after cutting, freeze–thaw).
  - **Preservation** comes either from fast enzyme denaturation (steam blanching, 120 °C for 8 min, boiling alcohol) or from keeping enzymes slow and water low (room-temperature shade drying, cold alcohol maceration).
- Cell-disrupting steps (grinding fresh leaves, freezing, thawing) are critical control points. A raw-material spec should state when, and how, enzymes were deactivated.
- Under-controlled drying cuts leaf oleuropein by about 2–6×: 10.0 vs 1.7 mg/g in the Palestinian data, and −57.7% after a 5-min thaw. That is comparable to or larger than the cultivar and seasonal effects in Q2.

### Gaps
- No quantitative leaf data were retrieved for steam/blanching or microwave drying in olive (only privet steaming, qualitatively), nor for olive-leaf PPO or esterase activity.
- Surfaced in summaries but not tied to a specific paper: fresh leaves showing *lower* extractable oleuropein than dried leaves because of β-glucosidase; oven drying at 105 °C "providing oleuropein … greater than 10% DW"; hot-air drying at 120 and 160 °C not differing from freeze-drying; delayed drying after harvest causing degradation.

## Q4. Extraction and purification routes, and what Phyto-C's described route likely yields compared with the 2004 patent's chloroform route

### Takeaway
Aqueous ethanol (about 50–80%) is consistently among the best solvents for oleuropein. Reported yields run from about 13 mg/g up to about 67 mg/g of leaf, and single-step dry extracts contain about 18–26% oleuropein, or 30–40% in a 1990s aqueous-alcohol patent. Pure supercritical CO2 extracts very little without a polar co-solvent. Macroporous resins raise purity to about 47–90%. The 2004 Pinnell & Omar patent (US 6,743,449 B2) prefers chloroform as its "non-aqueous solvent" step, adds an activated-charcoal/alcohol boil, and targets only **about 6–10% oleuropein** in the final extract. A well-run hydroalcoholic route is therefore expected to give an extract at least as rich in oleuropein (likely 2–4× higher per unit dry matter) if heat during concentration is controlled. The actual figure for Phyto-C's current extract is unknown.

### Cited Findings

### Inferences
- **Hydroalcoholic route** (the brief's description of Phyto-C's current process): from the cited data, one-step 50–80% ethanol extraction of well-dried leaves should give a dry extract of roughly 18–26% oleuropein (the CIMB 2026 range), up to about 30–40% (US 5,714,150), without resin purification. Leaf quality and enzyme control (Q3) set the upper limit.
- **Chloroform route**: oleuropein is a polar glycoside (water-soluble at about 9.5 g/L). The chloroform step in the 2004 patent most plausibly acts as a lipophilic wash, removing waxes, chlorophyll, triterpenes and sterols, rather than as the oleuropein carrier; the extracts did not reveal step a), so this is an inference. Its stated target of 6–10% is *lower* than routine hydroalcoholic dry extracts. Activated charcoal decolourises but can also adsorb phenolics (general adsorption chemistry, not measured here). Boiling in alcohol adds thermal load. The likely trade-off between the routes:
  - Chloroform route: cleaner colour and less lipophilic matter, at moderate oleuropein.
  - Hydroalcoholic route: higher oleuropein %, but more co-extracted chlorophyll and triterpenes if there is no defatting step, and no chlorinated-solvent residue concern.
- **"High-pressure evaporation"** is technically odd. Solvent removal is normally done under *reduced* pressure (vacuum) to lower the boiling point. If the phrase is literal, the relevant risk is temperature × time: oleuropein degrades by first-order kinetics that speed up sharply above about 40 °C (Q7). The concentration step's temperature, time and water activity matter more than the label.
- Without a CoA (oleuropein % by HPLC, dry-matter %, solvent residues, carrier), "proprietary SuperHeal" cannot be placed on this scale.

### Gaps
- Whether Perugini et al. 2008 used a **Naviglio extractor** could not be verified; the searches found no mention of Naviglio, and the Naviglio search was blocked by the budget.
- Retrieved values for microwave-assisted extraction, superheated water and activated-carbon purification of olive leaf were incomplete.
- Step a) of US 6,743,449 (first extraction solvent, leaf state, any harvest month), its worked-example yields, and whether 6–10% is on a dry or as-is basis were not retrieved.

## Q6. Analytics: HPLC-UV methods, reference standards, marker assay vs fingerprinting, and CoA reporting

### Takeaway
Oleuropein is quantified by reversed-phase HPLC with UV/DAD against an external standard (commercial standards ≥98%). Detection wavelengths used are 230, 238 and 280 nm. Fingerprinting (LC-MS/TOF, UV/MIR chemometrics) is used for identity and origin rather than potency. CoA conventions (dry vs as-is basis, wavelength, standard purity) could not be checked.

### Cited Findings
- A validated HPLC-DAD method exists for oleuropein and other bioactives in olive-leaf by-products — [PubMed 32839982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32839982/).
- HPLC with a photodiode-array detector monitoring oleuropein at 238 nm — [CIMB 2026;48(6):549](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13298046/).
- "The diodearray records were made at 280 nm and 230 nm" for oleuropein analysis in a patent LC-MS method — [US 8,574,635](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8574635).
- Reference standard ≥98.0% — [Sigma-Aldrich 12247](https://b2b.sigmaaldrich.com/US/en/product/sigma/12247).
- Fingerprinting: HPLC-ESI-TOF and ion-trap MS fingerprints with hierarchical cluster analysis separated Tunisian Chemlali leaves by region — [PMC5872618](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5872618/).
- UV spectroscopy plus multivariate analysis was used for quality assessment of 12 cultivars across seasons — [PubMed 33619785](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33619785/).
- Rapid quantitative determination of oleuropein in leaves by mid-infrared spectroscopy with chemometrics — [Academia 16832597](https://www.academia.edu/16832597/Rapid_quantitative_determination_of_oleuropein_in_olive_leaves_Olea_europaea_using_mid_infrared_spectroscopy_combined_with_chemometric_analyses).
- Review of analytical advances for olive-leaf phenolics; title only retrieved — [PMC4712944](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4712944/).

### Inferences
- A meaningful CoA for a cosmetic-grade extract should state:
  - oleuropein % by HPLC-UV, with the wavelength and standard purity;
  - the basis (dry extract vs as-is liquid);
  - dry matter / total solids;
  - solvent and carrier;
  - ideally hydroxytyrosol % (a degradation marker) and a fingerprint (verbascoside, luteolin-7-O-glucoside) for identity.
- A marker-only assay can hide degradation or blending. A rising hydroxytyrosol-to-oleuropein ratio signals hydrolysis.
- Wavelength choice affects specificity. Around 230–240 nm the secoiridoid (elenolic) chromophore is likely strong, while 280 nm is the generic phenolic band. This is spectroscopic reasoning; λmax/ε were not retrieved.

### Gaps
- Pharmacopoeial methods (Ph. Eur./USP), typical CoA templates, LOD/LOQ figures and inter-lab variability were not retrieved.

## Q7. Stability and degradation: hydrolysis, heat, light/UV, oxidation, storage, and behaviour in formulations

### Takeaway
Oleuropein degrades by first-order kinetics that depend strongly on temperature and humidity. In humid (75% RH) leaf powder the half-life is about 30 days at room temperature but about 13 h at 40 °C. Products are the aglycone, then hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid (enzymatic, acid or thermal routes). The "UV causes rapid decomposition" statement traces to UV-C irradiation of solutions, plus a patent's sun-exposure example on leaves. Under natural (indoor) light, stored leaf powder showed no significant light effect. Aqueous extracts last about a week at room temperature. No data on oleuropein in cosmetic emulsions or gels (half-lives, % retained by pH) were found.

### Cited Findings
**Kinetics**
- Antioxidants 2021, "Oleuropein Degradation Kinetics in Olive Leaf and Its Aqueous Extracts":
  - Degradation "conforms well to first-order kinetics".
  - Solid leaf powder at 75% RH: k = 0.023 day⁻¹ (t½ ≈ 30.1 days) at room temperature; k = 1.282 day⁻¹ (t½ ≈ 0.54 day ≈ 13 h) at 40 °C.
  - Aqueous extracts were held at 80, 60, 40, 24 ± 1.4 (RT), 14 and 7 °C, with **separate activation energies** for RT–80 °C and for 7–14 °C, because "the degradation reactions were different in these ranges".
  - Higher temperature and RH accelerate degradation, and low RH stabilises it.
  - "No significant differences (p > 0.05) between storage in darkness and storage in natural light", reported for the solid powder work.
  - [PMC8750354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8750354/); [Semantic Scholar PDF](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/354f/9011271b176f54277d8043a1859f5345db01.pdf)
- Extract storage, Malik & Bradford: oleuropein "was quite stable in aqueous extracts for 7 days when stored at room temperatures but degraded after 17 days". A methanol extract was reported stable for 30 days at room temperature (possibly a separate study) — [Malik & Bradford](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/43270134_Recovery_and_stability_of_oleuropein_and_other_phenolic_compounds_during_extraction_and_processing_of_olive_Olea_europaea_L_leaves).
- Storage temperature: −20 °C was the optimal storage condition for olive-leaf extracts and 25 °C the least desirable — [Academia 124244423, "Effect of storage, temperature, and pH on the preservation of the oleuropein content of olive leaf extracts"](https://www.academia.edu/124244423/Effect_of_storage_temperature_and_pH_on_the_preservation_of_the_oleuropein_content_of_olive_leaf_extracts).
- After 27 days the "degradation rate of oleuropein was 95.24% at 25 °C and 38.1% at 4 °C". Whether this means loss or residual is ambiguous in the extract — [J Renew Mater, Techscience v11n8](https://www.techscience.com/jrm/v11n8/53377/html).
- Thermogravimetry of solid oleuropein: decomposition activation energies 143.72 and 247.01 kJ/mol — [ScienceDirect S2164632523000951](https://www.sciencedirect.com/org/science/article/pii/S2164632523000951).

**Light and UV**
- "Effect of light irradiation on the antioxidant stability of oleuropein" (Food Chem 2017):
  - Oleuropein is fairly stable in water or ethanol, but under **UV-C** it breaks down quickly to hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid, by a solvent-dependent pathway.
  - Antioxidant activity held roughly steady in water over 150 min but fell in ethanol.
  - Lamp wavelength and irradiance and the rate constants were not in the extract.
  - [Food Chem 2017, S0308814617308981](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814617308981)
- A 2015 PubMed-indexed abstract states oleuropein can be degraded to hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid under light, acid, base or high temperature; the primary article was not pinned — [science.gov topic page](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/t/tyrosol+hydroxytyrosol+oleuropein.html).
- Sunlight on dried leaves: "The stability of the Oleuropein is also affected by sunlight. For an Oleuropein content of 3.5% in dry leaves after 12 hours of exposure to the sun at 20 °C the Oleuropein content falls to 0.82%" [−77%]. This was quoted in a search extract of the patent; a second search could not re-confirm it — [USPTO 6,676,980](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6676980).

**Hydrolysis products and enzymatic conversion**
- Enzymatic hydrolysis of olive-leaf extract with β-glucosidase gave a high concentration of oleuropein aglycone after 2 h — [Molecules, PMC6272143](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6272143/).
- Oleuropein-aglycone and hydroxytyrosol-rich hydrolysates have been prepared from Chemlali leaves — [Chem Biol Interact 2008, S0009279708004079](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0009279708004079).
- Titles only (data not retrieved): [Drying and storage of olive leaf extracts, Ind Crops Prod 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S092666901530501X); [thermal stability after cloud-point extraction, PMC4665490](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4665490/); [liposomal olive-leaf extract stability, PMC12655330](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655330).

### Inferences
- For a cosmetic product the dominant risks are **hydrolysis and thermal degradation in water-containing formulas held warm**, plus enzymatic conversion if the raw material was not enzyme-deactivated. The first-order data imply roughly 50-fold faster loss at 40 °C than at room temperature in humid powder. That has direct consequences for warm-climate shipping and for 40–45 °C accelerated stability tests.
- "UV light itself causes rapid decomposition" is supported **only for UV-C in solution**. UV-C does not reach the ground in sunlight (general photophysics, not from these sources), so this result is not representative of photostability in use. For UVA/UVB and natural light in-pack, the retrieved evidence is either indirect (the patent's outdoor sun example on leaves, which also involves heat and possible enzymes) or null (no indoor light effect on powder). Opaque, airless packaging remains prudent but is not proven necessary by these data.
- Acidic vitamin-C formulas, like the patent's example with 15–20% L-ascorbic acid, are strongly acidic (general formulation knowledge, not verified here). That would favour slow acid-catalysed hydrolysis of the glycosidic and ester bonds. No kinetic data in such a matrix were found.

### Gaps
- An activation energy of **98.03 ± 0.08 kJ/mol at pH 2.0** for oleuropein hydrolysis in aqueous olive-leaf extract surfaced in one summary, but the source paper could not be identified. Verify before use.
- Not found: base-catalysed kinetics; pH-rate profiles; numbers from the Antioxidants 2021 aqueous-extract table; any half-life or % retained for oleuropein in O/W emulsions, gels, serums or vitamin-C serums at different pH and temperatures; oxidation (PPO, metal) kinetics in formulations.

### Cited Findings
- MW 540.5 g/mol — [PubChem](https://pubchem.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/compound/5281544). Water solubility about 9.5 g/L — [PubMed 32839982](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32839982/).
- Perugini et al. 2008: an emulsion (and a second formulation type) containing oleuropein was applied to volunteers before UVB to test protection and after UVB to test soothing, with erythema assessed. Oleuropein was 288.6 µg/mL in the extract and 255.1 µg/mL in the final formulation — [Int J Cosmet Sci 2008, "Efficacy of oleuropein against UVB irradiation: preliminary evaluation"](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1468-2494.2008.00424.x); [Rev Ceres review](https://scielo.br/j/rceres/a/ZpwXDZP4RkK3Bg3bnfykrvP/?lang=en).
- In vitro: in HaCaT keratinocytes and monocytes, oleuropein lowered IL-6 by about 39–46% in monocytes and oxidative-stress cell death in HaCaT by about 6–9% — [PMC11751055](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11751055/). Sebocyte and keratinocyte effects of the extract and pure oleuropein are summarised in Q9 — [CIMB 2026](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13298046/).
- Animal UVB studies with olive-leaf extract and oleuropein exist (titles only; route not retrieved) — [J Nutr, hairless mice chronic UVB](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0022316622065889); [Phytother Res 2010, C57BL/6J acute UVB](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ptr.3048).
- A liposomal olive-leaf-extract carrier study exists (title only) — [PMC12655330](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12655330).

### Inferences
- At 540.5 Da oleuropein sits just above the ~500 Da guideline, and it is a hydrophilic glycoside. Both point to poor partitioning into stratum-corneum lipids and low passive flux from simple aqueous gels. This is physicochemical reasoning, not measured flux.
- Hydroxytyrosol, the main hydrolysis product (≈154 Da, C8H10O3; standard formula, not retrieved here), is much smaller. Partial hydrolysis in-pack or on skin could increase delivery of a phenolic active while lowering the label marker.
- Perugini's effect on UVB erythema at about 0.026% oleuropein shows *some* topical activity. It does not separate surface antioxidant or UV-absorbing effects from penetration, and "preliminary" design details (n, controls) were not retrieved.

### Gaps
- Not found: Franz-cell flux (human or porcine skin), lag times, amounts in stratum corneum, epidermis or dermis, tape-stripping, in-vivo microdialysis, and vehicle comparisons (emulsion vs gel vs liposomes, niosomes, nanoemulsions, ethosomes) for oleuropein or hydroxytyrosol. These need a follow-up search session.

## Q9. Verdicts on the specific claims: (1) degradation/UV, (2) the 2026 sebocyte/keratinocyte study, (3) Phyto-C's "October harvest = highest oleuropein"; also Phyto-C SuperHeal specs

### Takeaway
**(1) Partly true, overstated on UV.** Hydrolysis by enzymes, acid or heat to hydroxytyrosol + elenolic acid is well supported. The "rapid UV decomposition" source is UV-C in solution, and natural light had no significant effect on stored leaf powder. **(2) Found and largely confirmed.** The paper is DOI 10.3390/cimb48060549, i.e. MDPI *Curr. Issues Mol. Biol.* 2026;48(6):549 (journal inferred from the DOI prefix "cimb"). The 70% ethanol extract had the most oleuropein (25.73% vs 18.40% for "OLW", presumably water), and pure oleuropein reproduced key effects. But it is in-vitro and exploratory, uses immortalised cell lines, and the chemistry differences were moderate. **(3) Not supported as a general rule.** Most multi-month studies peak in February–March; October or autumn highs are cultivar- or site-specific. **SuperHeal spec: nothing published was found.**

### Cited Findings
**Claim (1): "degrades under light, acid, base and high temperature mainly to hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid; UV light itself causes rapid decomposition"**
- Supporting, near-verbatim: a 2015 PubMed-indexed abstract says oleuropein can be degraded to hydroxytyrosol and elenolic acid by light, acid, base or high temperature (primary article not pinned) — [science.gov](https://www.science.gov/topicpages/t/tyrosol+hydroxytyrosol+oleuropein.html).
- The UV claim's probable primary source: oleuropein is stable in water or ethanol without UV but breaks down quickly under **UV-C** to hydroxytyrosol + elenolic acid, by a solvent-dependent pathway — [Food Chem 2017, "Effect of light irradiation on the antioxidant stability of oleuropein"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0308814617308981).
- Sunlight on dried leaves: 3.5% fell to 0.82% after 12 h of sun at 20 °C (quoted in a search extract) — [USPTO 6,676,980](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6676980).
- Contrary or qualifying: no significant difference between darkness and natural light for stored leaf powder (p > 0.05); temperature and RH were the main drivers — [Antioxidants 2021, PMC8750354](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8750354/).
- Mechanism of the enzymatic route: β-glucosidase gives the aglycone (more than 50% hydrolysed in 5 min by recombinant fruit enzyme), and esterase then gives hydroxytyrosol + elenolic acid — [Front Plant Sci 2017](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/plant-science/articles/10.3389/fpls.2017.01902/full).

**Claim (2): the 2026 sebocyte/keratinocyte study**
- Citation: "Olive Leaf Extract Suppresses Sebogenesis and Inflammation via AKT/ERK and SREBP-1/PPAR-γ Signaling in Human Sebocytes," DOI 10.3390/cimb48060549, PMID 42353554, PMC13298046. The author list was not retrieved — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/cimb48060549); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42353554/); [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13298046/).
- Chemistry: oleuropein 18.40 ± 0.35% (OLW), 21.25 ± 0.14% (OLE50) and 25.73 ± 0.14% w/w (OLE70), by HPLC-PDA at 238 nm. OLE70 "exhibited the strongest antioxidant activity, the greatest gel contraction response, and the highest content of oleuropein" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.3390/cimb48060549).
- Bioactivity:
  - The extract "significantly inhibited linoleic acid-induced lipid accumulation in SEB-1 sebocytes without cytotoxicity", lowered AKT and ERK phosphorylation, and suppressed PPAR-γ and SREBP-1.
  - In HaCaT keratinocytes it reduced IL-8, TNF-α and PGE2 induced by *C. acnes* or UVB.
  - Oleuropein alone reproduced the sebum-lowering effect, the IL-8 inhibition and the PPAR-γ/SREBP-1 suppression.
  - The authors call the results "exploratory".
  - [PubMed 42353554](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42353554/); [PMC13298046](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13298046/)

**Claim (3): "October harvest = highest oleuropein"**
- Against: Spain, March or February peaks (107.17 / 102.49 / 71.95 mg/g) — [Agronomy 2021](https://doi.org/10.3390/agronomy11122447). Arbequina, pruning season — [Horticulturae 2022](https://doi.org/10.3390/horticulturae8060530). Croatia, March highest, low temperatures raise oleuropein, sampled October/January/March — [Plants 2020](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/9/9/1099); [Plants 2025](https://www.mdpi.com/2223-7747/14/17/2789). Pruning period above harvest period — [Plants 2020;9(12):1667](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7760844/). China, autumn decrease — [PMC6963812](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6963812/). Egypt, spring above autumn — [PubMed 33619785](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33619785/). Nizip (Turkey), winter highest — [Food Biosci 2024](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S2212429224019552). Lowest in 'Mission' autumn leaves — [J Herbmed Pharmacol](https://herbmedpharmacol.com/Article/jhp-45000).
- For: Ayvalık (Turkey), autumn highest at about 271.66 ppm, summer significantly lower — [PMC5430204](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5430204/). Spanish table olives, high plateau from August to October and lowest in April and December — [Foods 2022](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/11/23/3879).
- Cultivar dependence overrides season: in eleven cases even the *direction* of the seasonal change was cultivar-specific — [Plants 2025](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12430329/).

**Phyto-C SuperHeal**
- Brand pages give no oleuropein %, solvent or dry matter. They say only "patented … rich in Oleuropein" and cite US 6,743,449 B2 — [Phyto-C gel](https://www.phyto-c.com/products/superheal-o-live-gel); [Phyto-C blog](https://www.phyto-c.com/blogs/news/olive-leaf-extract-antioxidant-skincare-science).
- That patent's own target is about 6–10% oleuropein via a chloroform and activated-charcoal process — [US6743449B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6743449B2/en).

### Inferences
- Claim (1) is best restated: "oleuropein is hydrolysed (enzymatically, by acid or base, and thermally) to its aglycone and then hydroxytyrosol + elenolic acid; UV-C degrades it rapidly in solution; under ambient light the evidence is mixed, and heat and humidity are the dominant storage risks."
- Claim (2) is real, but its wording should be tempered:
  - The "clearly different chemistry" was a 1.4× spread in oleuropein (18.4% → 25.7%) among OLW (presumably water) and 50%/70% ethanol extracts, not radically different profiles; other compounds were not retrieved.
  - The cells are immortalised lines (SEB-1, HaCaT).
  - Nothing shows the effects survive topical delivery at cosmetic doses (see Q8).
  - It is unclear from the extracts whether every bioassay used OLE70 or compared all three extracts.
- On claim (3): if Phyto-C harvests in October, that may suit fruit-harvest logistics, but the literature would generally favour late-winter or spring pruning leaves and young leaves for maximum oleuropein. Drying and enzyme control (Q3) matter as much as month.

### Gaps
- Not found: the CIMB 2026 author list, extraction temperature, time and ratio, extract yields, oleuropein concentrations used in cells, and IC50 or effect sizes.
- Not found: any Phyto-C document stating the October claim, its leaf origin, its current process parameters ("hydroalcoholic + high-pressure evaporation"), or a CoA.
- Whether US 6,743,449 is still in force, or was reassigned to Dr. Omar or Phyto-C, was not checked.
