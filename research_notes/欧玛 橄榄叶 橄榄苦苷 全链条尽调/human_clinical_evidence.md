## Study A: Perugini P et al., "Efficacy of oleuropein against UVB irradiation: preliminary evaluation", Int J Cosmet Sci 2008;30:113–120. Bibliographic data, abstract, and claim-by-claim check

### Takeaway
Full-text excerpts confirm most of the design facts in the user's material:
- 10 women aged 20–30, Fitzpatrick II–III.
- 1 cm² test sites on the buttocks; individual MED; erythema induced with 2 MED.
- 3 volunteers discharged for MED >140 mJ/cm², leaving n = 7.

Not found in any accessible text: the vehicle-specific P values (0.018 / 0.043 / 0.128), the formulas, an HPLC method statement, a vehicle-only site, the 24 h / ~1 h timing of the soothing protocol, and any funding or COI statement.

### Inferences
- **Which protocol the percentages describe.** The "22 % / 35 % / 30 %" figures describe the lenitive (applied after erythema, "soothing") protocol. The abstract attributes them to the two formulations jointly. If the user's per-vehicle P values are correct (gel a* P = 0.128), the 22 % erythema reduction cannot be claimed for the emulsion gel. It would rest on the O/W emulsion.
- **What the study can support.** Its own positive control (tocopherol acetate) beat oleuropein on every parameter, and the authors call the protective effect "slight". The study supports at most "a weak, short-term effect of a single 0.1 % oleuropein application in an artificial UVB model, weaker than vitamin E". It does not support claims of superiority or clinically meaningful anti-redness effects.
- **Vehicle effects cannot be separated out.** No vehicle-only site was found. Emollient vehicles can lower TEWL and modify a* readings in irritated skin, so the TEWL and a* effects cannot be attributed to oleuropein itself. This is a mechanistic inference, not a finding of the paper.
- **The seven-vs-five-area conflict.** A plausible reconciliation is that seven graded-dose 1 cm² sites were the MED phototest, and about five sites per protocol were used for treatment. This is an inference only.
- **Possible dosimetry issue.** Measured MEDs (50–140 mJ/cm²) were higher than literature values, and three of ten subjects exceeded the tested range. This suggests lamp-spectrum or dosimetry differences, which limits comparison with other UVB-erythema studies.
- **Stability.** The emulsion's 37 °C value (82.68 ± 17.11) overlaps its own 20 °C value (92 ± 19.87). The gel lost about half its oleuropein. The authors' "low thermal stability in both" conclusion means shelf-life potency of oleuropein in cosmetic vehicles cannot be assumed from this paper.
- **Directness.** The model is single-application, 2-MED UVB on the buttocks of seven phototype II–III women. It is indirect evidence for facial persistent redness, rosacea, or post-procedure skin in Asian phototypes (III–V).
- **Conflict of interest.** A company (Lachifarma) appears among the author affiliations. That is a potential COI regardless of whatever funding statement the full text contains.

- All numeric results tables: means, SDs, per-vehicle and per-protocol values, P values. Effect sizes therefore cannot be computed.

## Study A: Wilcoxon signed-rank consistency check for n = 7

### Takeaway
The user's P values are exactly the values that an SPSS-style *asymptotic* Wilcoxon signed-rank test gives at n = 7 (normal approximation, no continuity correction):
- **0.018** means T = 0: all 7 subjects moved in the favourable direction.
- **0.043** means T = 2: 6 of 7 favourable. An alternative is 5 non-zero pairs with T = 0.
- **0.128** means T = 5: 5 or 6 of 7 favourable.

These values are internally consistent with the test the paper reports, but consistency is not verification. 0.018 is the *smallest* P this test can produce at n = 7. The exact-test minimum is 2/128 = 0.0156.

### Cited Findings
- The paper used the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-ranks test. Source: [PDF](https://www.olivelife.gr/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Efficacy-of-oleuropein-against-UVB-irradiation-preliminary-evaluation.pdf).
- Three of ten volunteers were discharged (MED >140 mJ/cm²), leaving 7 for analysis. Source: [PDF](https://www.olivelife.gr/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Efficacy-of-oleuropein-against-UVB-irradiation-preliminary-evaluation.pdf).

### Inferences
**My computation** (script: `/tmp/claude-0/-home-user-i18ns/a028ca4f-5eb0-59a0-a3b4-486bfae2ef0a/scratchpad/scripts/wilcoxon_n7.py`, run with `python3 -I`):
- For n = 7 with no zeros or ties, T is the smaller signed-rank sum.
- E[T] = n(n+1)/4 = 7·8/4 = **14**.
- Var[T] = n(n+1)(2n+1)/24 = 7·8·15/24 = **35**, so SD = **5.9161**.
- z = (T − 14)/5.9161, and the two-sided P = 2Φ(z).
- Exact two-sided P = 2·#{subsets of ranks 1..7 with sum ≤ T}/2⁷. The minimum is **2/128 = 0.015625**.

| T | z (no cc) | P, normal approx., no cc | P, normal approx. with 0.5 cc | P, exact |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | −2.3664 | **0.01796 → 0.018** | 0.02249 | 0.01563 (2/128) |
| 1 | −2.1974 | 0.02799 | 0.03461 | 0.03125 (4/128) |
| 2 | −2.0284 | **0.04252 → 0.043** | 0.05191 | 0.04688 (6/128) |
| 3 | −1.8593 | 0.06298 | 0.07593 | 0.07813 (10/128) |
| 4 | −1.6903 | 0.09097 | 0.10832 | 0.10938 (14/128) |
| 5 | −1.5213 | **0.12819 → 0.128** | 0.15079 | 0.15625 (20/128) |
| 6 | −1.3522 | 0.17630 | 0.20489 | 0.21875 (28/128) |
| 7 | −1.1832 | 0.23672 | 0.27190 | 0.29688 (38/128) |

**What each reported P value implies** (assuming no zero differences or tied ranks):
- **P = 0.018** implies T = 0: all 7 subjects favourable (e.g. TEWL and a* for the emulsion, per the user's material). No other n ≤ 7 can produce 0.018; for comparison, the n = 6 minimum is 0.0277.
- **P = 0.043** implies either:
  - n = 7 and T = 2: 6 of 7 favourable, with the one unfavourable difference ranked 2nd-smallest; or
  - two zero differences dropped (effective n = 5) and T = 0: P = 0.04311.

  Since LDF readings are continuous, the first reading is more likely.
- **P = 0.128** implies n = 7 and T = 5. That means either one unfavourable subject at rank 5 (6/7 favourable), or two unfavourable subjects at ranks {1, 4} or {2, 3} (5/7 favourable).
- **Only the asymptotic, no-continuity-correction variant matches.** The SPSS default "Asymp. Sig." is the only variant that reproduces all three values. The exact test would give 0.0156 / 0.0469 / 0.1563. The continuity-corrected approximation would give 0.0225 / 0.0519 / 0.1508.
- **Tied |differences|** would shrink the variance and push P slightly below 0.018 at T = 0. An exact 0.018 therefore suggests no tied ranks.

**Interpretation:**
- **Identical P values across endpoints and vehicles are expected.** At n = 7 only 8 distinct P values ≤ 0.25 exist, so repeats like 0.018/0.018 and 0.043/0.043 are not a red flag in themselves. P = 0.018 shows that the direction of change was consistent; it says nothing about magnitude.
- **No multiplicity correction could be survived.** The study had at least 12 comparisons (3 instruments × 2 vehicles × 2 protocols, plus the vitamin E arm). The exact-test minimum of 0.0156 is already above 0.05/4 = 0.0125. The asymptotic minimum of 0.018 is above 0.05/3 = 0.0167. Under any Bonferroni-type correction for this design, **no P value in the study could be significant**.

### Gaps
- The raw per-subject data, the paired comparison actually used (treated vs irradiated-untreated site, or post vs pre), and how ties or zeros were handled were not retrievable.

### Inferences
- **Design.** The study was uncontrolled and open-label: every participant used the cream and no comparator appears in the abstract. Changes against baseline cannot be attributed to the product, let alone to the olive leaf extract within it. Seasonal change, regression to the mean, a Hawthorne effect and the moisturising vehicle are all alternative explanations.
- **Relevance to Phyto-C gels.** The product was a multi-ingredient cream. The user's material says it contains ceramide (unverified), and attributing the TEWL and hydration effects to OLE rather than to emollients or ceramide is not possible. The tested SKU was the **O-Live Cream**, so the result cannot be transferred to the O-Live **Gel** or to any other Phyto-C product.
- **TEWL at 1 month.** The abstract reports significant TEWL change only at 2 months and at the post-treatment follow-up, not at 1 month. This suggests the 1-month TEWL change was not significant (inference).

  | Erythema index, 2 months | −5.2 | −2.1 % | 0.10 |
  | Erythema index, follow-up | −9.2 | −3.7 % | 0.17 |

  On these numbers, the cream did **not** reduce erythema in this population.
- **Where P = 1.000 most likely comes from.** P = 1.000 for a non-zero mean change is characteristic of **Bonferroni-adjusted pairwise comparisons**, where the adjusted P is min(1, k × raw P). In SPSS this typically follows a repeated-measures ANOVA or Friedman test. With 4 time points:
  - if all 6 pairwise comparisons were adjusted (the SPSS repeated-measures default), the follow-up P of 0.703 implies a raw P ≈ 0.117, and 1.000 implies a raw P ≥ 0.167;
  - if only the 3 comparisons against baseline were adjusted, the raw P values are ≈ 0.234 and ≥ 0.333.

  This is an inference; the method section was not accessible.

### Gaps
- Full text. Needed for: all baseline and follow-up means ± SD; the statistical methods; the population (sex, age, phototype, season of enrolment); the dose (~0.6 g); the washout and concomitant-product rules; drop-outs and n analysed; adverse events; limitations; the ceramide statement; funding, sponsor role and the instrument lender; and the product composition or INCI.
- PMID, issue number and full author list.
- **Unverified adverse-event lead.** One search digest said a non-primary summary page reported that "16.7% of the participants experienced mild and transient acneiform eruptions during the treatment period". 16.7 % of 36 is exactly 6 participants, which makes it plausible that the figure comes from the paper. The page's URL could not be identified, so check this against the full text before using it.
- **Unresolved conflict.** One search digest attributed to "a later review" the statement that the cream "reduced the area and depth of wrinkles, although not significantly". That contradicts the abstract's "Wrinkles improved significantly on all follow-ups". Which review said this (possibly the 2022 *Food & Function* review listed below) could not be confirmed.

## Other human studies of topical OLE, oleuropein, hydroxytyrosol or olive polyphenols, and of Phyto-C products

### Takeaway
The searches I could run found **no human skin trial of topical OLE, oleuropein, hydroxytyrosol or olive polyphenols other than Studies A and B**. Every other olive-leaf skin paper that surfaced was in vitro or animal work. Study B is the **only identified human study of any Phyto-C/PhytoCeuticals product** (SUPERHEAL™ O-Live Cream). ## Rosacea and persistent facial erythema: does any randomized vehicle-controlled trial of oleuropein or OLE exist?

### Takeaway
**No randomized, vehicle-controlled trial of oleuropein or olive leaf extract in rosacea, sensitive skin or persistent facial erythema was identified.** The human data that touch erythema at all are:
- Study A: acute UVB-induced erythema on buttock skin, n = 7, no vehicle-only site found, vitamin E reference superior to oleuropein.
- Study B: erythema index in photoaged faces, uncontrolled. The abstract reports no erythema effect, and the user's own figures show a 0.7–3.7 % change with P = 1.000, 1.000 and 0.703.

Because the registries could not be searched, the precise statement is "none found in a limited search".

### Cited Findings
- Study A tested acute UVB erythema (2 MED) on the buttocks of 10 women (n = 7 analysed). Vitamin E was "most effective … for all parameters", and the clinical redness decrease was "not significant". Source: [PDF](https://www.olivelife.gr/wp-content/uploads/2021/08/Efficacy-of-oleuropein-against-UVB-irradiation-preliminary-evaluation.pdf).
- Study B's abstract lists erythema index among its primary outcomes but reports significant results only for TEWL, hydration, wrinkles and self-rated texture. Source: [Wiley abstract](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/jocd.13457).

### Inferences
- **Directness.** Acute UVB erythema (an inflammatory sunburn model) and rosacea or persistent erythema (neurovascular dysregulation, vessel dilation, barrier impairment) are different conditions. A slight effect in the former cannot stand in for the latter.
- **What claims are supportable.** Any marketing or medical claim that olive leaf extract or oleuropein products "reduce redness" or "treat rosacea" is **unsupported by controlled human evidence** in what was retrievable. The only facial erythema data point to no change.

### Gaps
- No registry search: ClinicalTrials.gov, TCTR, ChiCTR, ISRCTN, CTRI, EU CTR.
- No search of rosacea-specific literature or conference abstracts for olive-derived actives.

## Evidence grading by endpoint (topical unless noted)

### Takeaway
The certainty of human evidence is **very low or absent for every endpoint**:
- Acute UV erythema: one tiny within-subject study with a slight effect, weaker than vitamin E.
- Barrier/TEWL: two weak signals, both confounded by vehicle effects.
- Photoaging: one uncontrolled pilot.
- Persistent redness/rosacea: no evidence, and an indirect null signal.
- Post-procedure healing: no evidence.
- Acne/sebum: no efficacy evidence, plus an unverified possible acneiform signal.
- Pigmentation: no efficacy evidence, plus an in vitro pro-melanogenic counter-signal.

### Cited Findings
- Study A's design, n, results, the vitamin E comparison and the stability data (as detailed above).
