# Cosmetic safety assessment and skin tolerance record of Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract and oleuropein (consumer dossier for an olive-leaf soothing gel, Phyto-C / 欧玛 O-Live Gel)

Research date: 2026-10-24. **How this was checked:** direct page fetching was blocked. WebFetch to `www.cir-safety.org` failed with "getaddrinfo ENOTFOUND", so no host was retried. Every claim below was checked **only through web-search result extracts** of the cited pages. No full PDF or abstract was read end to end. Text in quotation marks is wording as the search extracts returned it. Extracts can paraphrase or run fragments together, so the exact wording of the primary documents is still unverified. "(3P)" marks data taken from a third-party mirror or database rather than the issuing authority. ## 1. CIR assessment of Olea europaea (olive)-derived ingredients: title, years, conclusion, use concentrations, human patch-test, irritation and sensitization data

### Takeaway
CIR's Expert Panel for Cosmetic Ingredient Safety assessed 23 olive-derived ingredients in 2022–2023 under the title "Safety Assessment of Olea europaea (Olive)-Derived Ingredients":
- **Timeline:** literature review dated September 2022, Tentative Report June 2023, Final Report adopted at the September 11–12, 2023 meeting and released October 2, 2023.
- **Conclusion (split):** 16 fruit-, leaf-, husk- and seed-derived ingredients, including Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract, are safe in cosmetics in the present practices of use and concentration. For the 7 bark, branch, bud, flower, sap and wood ingredients, the data are insufficient.
- **Use:** leaf extract is the most frequently used olive ingredient (182 uses in 2022 VCRP data, 170 in 2023 VCRP data), mostly in leave-on skin care. Its maximum reported leave-on concentration is 2%, in suntan preparations.
- **Human data:** the human studies summarized found no irritation (including a 4-week in-use test of a moisturizer with 0.047% leaf extract, in which about half the subjects had sensitive skin). They found no sensitization for products containing 0.3%, 5% and 20% leaf extract.

**Frequency of use (FDA VCRP) and concentration of use (Council survey)**
- **2022 VCRP data:** "Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract has the highest frequency of use", with **182 formulations**, most of them leave-on skin care products (Table 6). — [CIR SLR Sept 2022](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/SLR_Olive_092022.pdf); [CIR Olive_2.pdf](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/Olive_2.pdf)
- **2023 VCRP data (June 2023 Tentative Report):** **170 formulations**, again mostly leave-on skin care (Table 7). — [CIR Tentative Report June 2023](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/TR_Olive_062023.pdf)
- **Maximum leave-on concentration:** "In the 2020 and 2023 use surveys, Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract has the highest concentration of use in a leave-on formulation; it is used at up to **2% in suntan preparations**." — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf); [CIR Tentative Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/TR_Olive_062023.pdf)
- **Maximum rinse-off concentration, any olive ingredient:** 10%, for Olea Europaea (Olive) Fruit Unsaponifiables in shaving cream. This is not the leaf extract. — [CIR Olive_2.pdf](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/Olive_2.pdf); [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- **Rinse-off figure for the leaf extract itself:** two Chinese compliance databases (3P) summarize CIR as safe at no more than **0.3% rinse-off and 2% leave-on**. The original Chinese wording is 「浓度不高于（淋洗类为：0.3 %）（驻留类为：2 %）时，该原料用于化妆品是安全的」 ("safe for cosmetic use at concentrations not exceeding 0.3% in rinse-off and 2% in leave-on"). The 0.3% rinse-off value did not appear in any CIR extract. — [hzpwjc.cn database (3P)](http://www.hzpwjc.cn/cir/inci2021/08138.html); [lv-1.cn database (3P)](https://www.lv-1.cn/yuanliao/inci/01808138010.html)
- **Face/neck products:** a leaf-extract maximum specific to face and neck products was **not found** in any extract.

**Human irritation, sensitization and photo-tests summarized by CIR for the leaf extract**
- **Irritation, 0.047% moisturizer (52 subjects):**
  - Design: a 4-week clinical safety-in-use test, with dermatological exams at baseline, week 2 and week 4. About half the subjects were considered to have sensitive skin.
  - Result: "Test material did not elicit any significant objective or subjective irritation."
  - Source cited by CIR: unpublished 2002 study, "Clinical safety in use test - moisturizer containing 0.047% Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract" (ref. 89). — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- **Irritation, 1% formulation:** a human dermal irritation study found no irritation. — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- **Sensitization:** "Dermal sensitization was also not observed in a maximization study of a lip balm containing 5% Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract (25 subjects), a product containing 20% Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract (54 subjects), or a product containing 0.3% Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract (109 subjects)." — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
  - **Unresolved test design:** one extract reports that the summary-table row for the 20% product (54 subjects) is labelled an **HRIPT**. The sentence above groups it with a maximization study. Whether the 20% and 0.3% studies were HRIPTs or maximization tests could not be confirmed.
- **Photo-test:** a human photocontact allergenicity study of a product with 10% leaf extract (25 subjects) found it was not a photosensitizer. Details are in Section 3. — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- **Not the leaf extract:** a patch test of a body scrub containing olive *seed powder* gave a "+" response in 1 of 21 subjects. — [CIR Olive_2.pdf](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/Olive_2.pdf)

**Animal, in vitro and systemic data**
- **Irritation, rabbits:** Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract "was not a dermal irritant in primary or cumulative skin irritation tests when tested at up to 100%". — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- **Irritation, in vitro:** an in vitro primary skin irritation study at 100% is listed. Its result was not shown in the extract. — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- **Sensitization, guinea pigs:** "In another guinea pig sensitization study, Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract was negative for sensitization when tested at up to 100% for both induction and challenge phases." — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- **Genotoxicity, aqueous leaf extracts:** a search extract described the extracts as mostly negative, with a borderline result for one of four extracts tested. It could not be confirmed whether this statement comes from the CIR text or from the underlying study. — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf); [In vitro toxicity/genotoxicity of aqueous leaf extracts from four Olea europaea varieties (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5407118/)
- **Bonolive, a proprietary water-soluble leaf extract (oral data):** "A Comprehensive Toxicological Safety Assessment of an Extract of Olea Europaea L. Leaves (Bonolive™)", Int J Toxicol 2016 (Clewell et al.). The extract was not mutagenic in bacterial and in vitro tests. The 90-day rat NOAEL (no-observed-adverse-effect level) was 1000 mg/kg bw/day, the highest dose tested. — [Clewell et al. 2016, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/1091581815619764); [PubMed 26658007](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26658007/)
- **In vitro skin-cell viability, olive fruit and leaf waste extracts:** viability problems at the highest dose tested (1000 μg/mL). The authors suggested the extracts are safe for skin application only up to 100 μg/mL. — [Olive Fruit and Leaf Wastes as Bioactive Ingredients for Cosmetics (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7914505/)

### Inferences
- **The concentration ceiling.** CIR's "safe" finding for leaf extract is tied to "present practices of use and concentration", and the documented leave-on maximum is 2%. A soothing gel at or below a few percent is inside, or near, the conditions CIR reviewed.
- **Higher concentrations.** Human tests at higher concentrations were negative: sensitization up to 20% and photocontact allergenicity at 10%. These give some margin, but they are single, unpublished, small studies summarized by CIR.
- **Tolerance on reactive skin.** The 0.047% safety-in-use test enrolled about 50% sensitive-skin subjects. That is the most consumer-relevant tolerance datum found for a leave-on face product, although 0.047% is a very low concentration.
- **What CIR did not assess.** CIR evaluated each extract as a whole; it did not evaluate oleuropein as an individual compound. Extracts sold under the same INCI name can differ widely in oleuropein content, solvent and carrier, so the test data apply most directly to the materials actually tested.

### Gaps
- **Exact conclusion sentence:** verbatim text from the Final Report could not be read, because cir-safety.org is unreachable. The wording above is from search extracts.
- **Face/neck, eye-area and rinse-off maxima** for the leaf extract in the Council survey table: not found. The 0.3% rinse-off figure is from Chinese third-party summaries only.
- **Test design of the 20% (n=54) and 0.3% (n=109) sensitization studies:** HRIPT or maximization is ambiguous in the extracts. Study sponsors and years were not found.
- **Journal publication:** no International Journal of Toxicology publication of the olive report was found as of 2026-10-24.

## 2. Published case reports of allergic or irritant contact dermatitis, urticaria or other skin reactions to olive leaf extract or other olive-tree material; relevance of olive pollen allergy to topical leaf extracts

### Takeaway
- **Leaf extract:** across multiple targeted searches, **no published case report** of allergic or irritant contact dermatitis, contact urticaria or other skin reaction attributed to olive leaf extract (cosmetic or herbal) was found.
- **Other olive-tree material:** skin reactions are documented, but they are rare and mostly old:
  - olive **wood**: allergic contact dermatitis (ACD), including one 1981 case from jewelry;
  - olive **oil**: ACD, including occupational cases;
  - olive **fruit**: contact urticaria and contact dermatitis.
- **Pollen:** Ole e 1 is the major olive *pollen* allergen. No source was found documenting a skin reaction to topical leaf extract in pollen-allergic people.

### Cited Findings
- **Olive wood, case report (Arch Dermatol 1981):**
  - "A case of allergic contact dermatitis to olive wood occurred. To our knowledge, this is the first such case reported." Patch tests with olive-wood scrapings were positive, and quinoid compounds were implicated.
  - The authors add that "compared with teakwood and rosewood, olive wood can only be considered a relatively weak sensitizer".
  — [PubMed 7316534](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7316534/); [JAMA Dermatology archive](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamadermatology/article-abstract/542648)
- **Olive oil, two patients (Joost et al., Contact Dermatitis 1981):** "a delayed type of hypersensitivity to pure freshly-prepared olive oil could be demonstrated in two patients". — [Contact Dermatitis 1981, Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1600-0536.1981.tb04086.x)
- **Olive oil, occupational case (2006):** "Occupational allergic contact dermatitis from olive oil" (PubMed 16958929). Extracts describe occupational hand eczema in a massage therapist linked to olive oil, with a recommendation to patch test workers exposed to vegetable oils. One extract also summarizes the literature view that olive oil acts more often as "a weak irritant rather than a contact sensitizer". — [PubMed 16958929](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16958929/); [ResearchGate copy](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6831298_Occupational_allergic_contact_dermatitis_from_olive_oil)
- **Olive fruit oil, CIR view (2017 fatty-acid-oils report):** sensitization to Olea Europaea (Olive) Fruit Oil "is considered rare". The case reports that exist "generally involved patients with venous eczema, some type of dermatitis or lesion, or an occupational exposure". — [CIR Plant-Derived Fatty Acid Oils](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/pdf/10.1177/1091581817740569)
- **Olive fruit, contact urticaria:** a PubMed record titled "Contact urticaria to olives" exists. Its details were not retrieved. — [PubMed 17177717](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17177717/)
- **Olive oil, review letter (Allergologia et Immunopathologia, 2011):** olive oil "has been implicated in allergic contact dermatitis, contact urticaria and allergic airway disease". Most sensitized patients were exposed through topical treatments. — [Allergol Immunopathol 2011](https://www.elsevier.es/en-revista-allergologia-et-immunopathologia-105-pdf-download-S0301054611002394)
- **Botanical Dermatology Database entry for Olea:**
  - wood reported to cause dermatitis (McCord 1958; Senear 1933);
  - contact dermatitis of the fingers from olives (Waldbott & Shea 1948);
  - contact dermatitis from olive oil, "which is often adulterated" (Greenberg & Lester 1954).
  - The extracted text did not mention the leaves.
  — [BoDD: Olea](https://www.botanical-dermatology-database.info/BotDermFolder/OLEA.html)
- **Negative example:** lip dermatitis from a wooden recorder made partly of olive wood. Patch testing pointed to African blackwood and rosewood, not olive. — [PMC6881855](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6881855/)
- **Oral herbal use of olive leaf:**
  - EMA HMPC (the European Medicines Agency's herbal committee) adopted a Community herbal monograph on *Olea europaea* L., folium on 22 November 2011. That version is now superseded. EMA's product page lists "Consultation - Revision 1" with status "C: ongoing call for scientific data". — [EMA superseded monograph PDF](https://www.ema.europa.eu/en/documents/herbal-monograph/superseded-community-herbal-monograph-olea-europaea-l-folium-first-version_en.pdf); [EMA Oleae folium page](https://www.ema.europa.eu/fr/medicines/herbal/oleae-folium)
  - An Egyptian Drug Authority monograph lists undesirable effects as "None known" and cites EMA/HMPC/359238/2016. This is a secondary source; the EMA's own undesirable-effects text was not retrieved. — [EDA Egypt monograph](https://www.edaegypt.gov.eg/media/movh3lt1/olea-europaea-l.pdf)
- **Olive pollen allergen Ole e 1:**
  - It is the main marker for primary olive-pollen allergy, and one source estimates it accounts for more than 80% of olive-pollen sensitization.
  - Cross-reactivity is mainly within the Oleaceae family (ash, privet, lilac); sequence similarity to non-Oleaceae homologs is 30–44%.
  — [Thermo Fisher allergen encyclopedia, Ole e 1](https://www.thermofisher.com/phadia/us/en/resources/allergen-encyclopedia/t9/t224.html); [Thermo Fisher, olive pollen t9](https://www.thermofisher.com/phadia/us/en/resources/allergen-encyclopedia/t9.html); [Olive–grass pollen cross-reactivity (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4045862/)
- **Pollen-to-fruit food allergy:** a patient had palatal itching and generalized urticaria after eating olives, 3 years after a diagnosis of olive pollinosis. This involved ingestion of the fruit, not skin contact with leaf extract. — [PubMed 20128426 "Food allergy due to olive"](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20128426/)
- **Negative search results:**
  - No search result linked topical olive leaf extract or cosmetics to reactions in olive-pollen-allergic people.
  - No case report of urticaria, anaphylaxis or rash from olive leaf extract was found.
  — [search extract set incl. LTP olive-fruit review](https://journals.co.za/doi/pdf/10.10520/ejc-caci-v34-n2-a6)

### Inferences
- **Absence of reports.** Despite at least 170–182 US formulations containing the leaf extract, no published case report blamed on it was found. This suggests the clinical allergy burden is low. Absence of reports is not proof of absence: plant extracts in multi-ingredient products are rarely patch tested individually.
- **Who should be cautious.** People with a known olive-oil ACD, olive-fruit contact urticaria or olive-wood allergy have a theoretical reason to test a leaf-extract gel on a small skin area (such as the forearm) before facial use. Shared olive constituents in leaf and other tree parts are biologically plausible but undocumented for leaf extract.
- **Pollen allergy.** Ole e 1 is a pollen protein. Whether leaf extracts contain it, or other relevant proteins such as lipid transfer proteins, was not documented, so pollen allergy alone has no evidence base as a contraindication. The only documented cross-over found is pollen to fruit, by ingestion.

### Gaps
- No PubMed or Contact Dermatitis/Dermatitis/JEADV case report specific to olive leaf extract was found. The full text of PubMed 17177717 and 16958929 was not retrieved (years and journals for these two are partly from extracts).
- No study on protein or Ole e allergen content of cosmetic olive leaf extracts was found.
- The EMA monograph's undesirable-effects text was not retrieved directly.

## 3. Photosafety data (phototoxicity, photoallergy) for olive leaf extract or oleuropein

### Takeaway
- **Human photoallergy:** the only human photosafety datum found is the CIR-summarized photocontact allergenicity test of a product with **10% leaf extract** (25 subjects). It was negative (not a photosensitizer).
- **Phototoxicity:** no in vitro phototoxicity test (such as the 3T3 NRU assay) and no human phototoxicity study for olive leaf extract or oleuropein was found.
- **Other studies:** published work on the leaf extract is about *photoprotection* (UV absorption at about 280 and 325 nm, and reduced UV damage in cell and human tests), not phototoxic hazard.

### Cited Findings
- **CIR photocontact allergenicity test:** a product containing 10% Olea Europaea (Olive) Leaf Extract did not act as a photosensitizer in 25 subjects. A separate product with 0.01% olive *fruit* extract was tested in 27 subjects. The extract was fragmentary, so the protocol (UV source, number of induction exposures) was not confirmed. — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf); [CIR Olive_2.pdf reference list](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/Olive_2.pdf)
- **UV absorption:** an olive leaf extract standardized to oleuropein showed two absorption bands, "Band II (λ_max 280 nm) and Band I (λmax 325 nm)", and was studied as a sunscreen booster. — [Photoprotection assessment of olive leaves extract standardized to oleuropein (ScienceDirect)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1011134418312466); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/331704942_Photoprotection_assessment_of_olive_Olea_europaea_L_leaves_extract_standardized_to_oleuropein_In_vitro_and_in_silico_approach_for_improved_sunscreens)
- **UVA damage in skin cells (Molecules 2022):** a standardized leaf extract reduced UVA-induced DNA damage and reactive oxygen species overproduction in human skin fibroblasts. This is a protective finding, not a phototoxicity test. — [Molecules 2022 (PMC9415354)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9415354/)
- **Human topical oleuropein after UVB (Perugini et al., Int J Cosmet Sci 2008;30:113–120):**
  - Design: an oleuropein emulsion and an emulgel were applied to healthy volunteers' UVB-irradiated skin, with vitamin E as the reference compound. The trade press reports 10 women aged 20–30.
  - Results when applied 24 hours after irradiation: erythema fell by about 22% and blood flow by about 30%. The fall in water loss is reported as 35% in the abstract and in MDedge's summary, but 25% in the trade press. This discrepancy is unresolved.
  - The pre-irradiation (protective) effect was slight and not significant.
  - None of the extracts mentioned adverse skin reactions; that is absence of mention, not a tolerance endpoint.
  — [Univ. Pavia repository](https://iris.unipv.it/handle/11571/140790); [CosmeticsDesign 2008](https://www.cosmeticsdesign.com/Article/2008/03/17/Olive-extract-could-soothe-skin-after-UV-damage); [MDedge oleuropein review](https://www.mdedge.com/dermatology/article/11562/aesthetic-dermatology/oleuropein/page/0/1)
- **Context only:** a 2025 study screening botanical cosmetic ingredients with the in vitro 3T3 NRU phototoxicity test found 5 of 38 botanicals had phototoxic potential. The extract did not identify olive among them, and olive-specific results were not found. — [PubMed 40935006](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40935006/)

### Inferences
- **Why phototoxicity data matter here.** Oleuropein absorbs in the UVB/UVA-II range (280/325 nm), so a phototoxicity screen would be relevant in principle.
- **What the evidence shows.** The available human photoallergy test (10%, negative) and the absence of photo-reaction case reports point to low photo-risk. That rests on one small unpublished study.

### Gaps
- No in vitro 3T3 NRU phototoxicity (OECD TG 432) and no human phototoxicity data were found for olive leaf extract, oleuropein or hydroxytyrosol.
- The protocol details of the CIR-summarized 10% photocontact allergenicity study were not found.

## 4. EU CosIng entry for Olea Europaea Leaf Extract; EFSA opinions giving safety context (hydroxytyrosol, olive polyphenols, olive leaf extract, novel-food status)

### Takeaway
- **CosIng (via mirrors):** the EU inventory lists OLEA EUROPAEA LEAF EXTRACT (CosIng ref. 35644) with the functions **Perfuming** and **Skin conditioning**. No annex restriction surfaced in the mirrors. The official CosIng page was not reachable.
- **Hydroxytyrosol:** EFSA found synthetic hydroxytyrosol safe as a novel food in 2017, and approved an LDL-protection health claim for olive polyphenols in 2011.
- **Olive leaf extract:** EFSA rejected a glucose-tolerance claim for an olive leaf water extract in 2014. In 2020 it could not conclude on the safety of an olive-leaf feed additive because the extract was poorly characterized.
- **Novel-food status:** traditional olive leaves and non-concentrated leaf infusions are reportedly "not novel".

### Cited Findings
- **CosIng record (3P mirrors):**
  - Skinsort: INCI OLEA EUROPAEA LEAF EXTRACT, CosIng ID 35644, functions "Perfuming, Skin Conditioning". — [Skinsort (3P)](https://skinsort.com/ingredients/olea-europaea-leaf-extract)
  - Cosmile Europe: functions fragrance and skin conditioning; plant olive, plant part leaves, preparation a solvent extract. — [Cosmile Europe (3P)](https://cosmileeurope.eu/inci/detail/9694/olea-europaea-leaf-extract/)
  - CosIng Checker: description "extract of the leaves of the olive, Olea europaea L., Oleaceae". — [CosIng Checker 35644 (3P)](https://cosingchecker.com/ingredients/35644-olea-europaea-leaf-extract)
  - No annex or restriction entry was shown in any mirror extract.
- **EFSA 2017, hydroxytyrosol novel food (EFSA Journal 2017, DOI 10.2903/j.efsa.2017.4728; NDA Panel):**
  - Conclusion: "The Panel concluded that the novel food, hydroxytyrosol, is safe under the proposed uses and use levels."
  - The assessment was based on a subchronic oral NOAEL of **50 mg/kg bw/day**. The margin of exposure is 100 for children aged 3–9 and at least 200 for adolescents and adults.
  - Proposed uses: fish and vegetable oils up to 215 mg/kg and margarines up to 175 mg/kg. Children under 36 months and pregnant or breastfeeding women are excluded.
  - EFSA's record gives the adoption date as 31 January 2017. A blog gives 25 January and a 5 mg/kg "safe upper bound"; it is considered unreliable.
  — [EFSA Journal 4728](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4728); [PMC7010075](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7010075/)
- **EFSA 2011, olive polyphenols health claim (EFSA Journal 2011;9(4):2033, per secondary sources):**
  - "The Panel concludes that a cause and effect relationship has been established between the consumption of olive oil polyphenols (standardised by the content of hydroxytyrosol and its derivatives) and protection of LDL particles from oxidative damage."
  - The condition of use is **5 mg/day** of hydroxytyrosol and its derivatives.
  - The quoted wording comes from trade-press extracts; the EFSA text itself was not read.
  — [FoodIngredientsFirst](https://www.foodingredientsfirst.com/news/efsa-validates-creagri-claims-about-olive-polyphenol-efficacy-in-protecting-ldl-particles-from-oxidative-damage.html); [Prepared Foods](https://preparedfoods.com/articles/110362-efsa-approves-heart-health-claim-for-olive-oil-polyphenol)
- **EFSA 2014, olive leaf water extract and glucose tolerance (DOI 10.2903/j.efsa.2014.3655; applicant Comvita):**
  - The extract, standardized by oleuropein content, was considered sufficiently characterized.
  - The single positive human study was not replicated. Olive Oil Times reports the claim was rejected for insufficient evidence.
  - The exact EFSA conclusion wording was not retrieved.
  — [AGRIS record](https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/6759761dc7a957febdf78ee4); [Olive Oil Times](https://oliveoiltimes.com/health-news/panel-rejects-health-claim-for-olive-leaf-extract/39765)
- **EFSA 2020, FEEDAP Panel on a dried aqueous-ethanol olive leaf extract used as a sensory feed additive:**
  - "As a full analysis of the extract was not provided, about 70% of the extract remained uncharacterised. In view of the inadequate chemical and toxicological characterisation of the additive, the FEEDAP Panel is unable to conclude on the safety for the target species, the consumers and the users."
  - Environment: use "is not expected to pose a risk for the environment".
  — [EFSA Journal 2020, 6018 (Wiley)](https://efsa.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.2903/j.efsa.2020.6018); [EFSA page](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/6018); [PMC7448074](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7448074/)
- **EU novel-food status (secondary industry summary of the Commission catalogue):**
  - Not novel: "traditional olive fruit, leaves, buds and non-concentrated aqueous leaf infusions remain 'not novel' based on documented pre-1997 consumption in the EU".
  - Novel: aqueous olive *fruit* extracts with at least 10% hydroxytyrosol.
  - Concentrated leaf extract: status not confirmed. A standardized olive leaf extract (Bonolive) was submitted for novel-food approval to the UK advisory committee (ACNFP) under Regulation 258/97 around 2016.
  — [Freyr Solutions](https://www.freyrsolutions.com/consumer-industry-news/eu-reconfirms-olive-fruit-extract-with-10-hydroxytyrosol-as-unauthorized-novel-food-in-foods-and-supplements); [ACNFP Bonolive dossier](https://acnfp.food.gov.uk/sites/default/files/bonolive.nonconf.pdf)

### Inferences
- **No EU restriction found.** No EU cosmetics restriction surfaced for the leaf extract (it appears only as an inventory entry with functions). Inventory listing in CosIng is not a safety approval.
- **Scope of the EFSA opinions.** They address oral exposure and food use, not skin. They offer only systemic-toxicity reassurance, for example that ingested hydroxytyrosol has a NOAEL of 50 mg/kg bw/day, which is far above any plausible absorption from a facial gel.
- **Recurring characterization problem.** The 2020 FEEDAP opinion shows that regulators treat poorly characterized olive leaf extracts as an unknown. This is the same point as in Section 1: data from one extract do not transfer automatically to another.

### Gaps
- The official CosIng page (functions, annex status, update date) was not reachable; mirrors only.
- The EFSA 2014 conclusion wording and the EU Novel Food Catalogue entry for concentrated olive leaf extract were not retrieved.
- No SCCS (the EU Scientific Committee on Consumer Safety) opinion on olive leaf extract, oleuropein or hydroxytyrosol was found.

## 5. China IECIC 2021 (已使用化妆品原料目录 2021年版): entry for 油橄榄（OLEA EUROPAEA）叶提取物 and maximum historical use levels

### Takeaway
- **Entry:** third-party Chinese databases (3P) report that the IECIC 2021 (China's Inventory of Existing Cosmetic Ingredients) lists 油橄榄（OLEA EUROPAEA）叶提取物 (olive leaf extract) as **No. 08138**.
- **Leave-on:** maximum historical use level **32%**.
- **Rinse-off:** **not found**.
- The official NMPA (China's medical products regulator) document could not be retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- The entry is listed as serial number 08138, with leave-on maximum historical use 32%. The original Chinese reads 「该原料已收录于《已使用化妆品原料目录（2021年版）》序号为08138；驻留类产品最高历史使用量为32%」 ("this ingredient is included in the IECIC 2021 as serial number 08138; maximum historical use level in leave-on products 32%"). The same page lists CIR reference levels of 2% leave-on and 0.3% rinse-off. — [hzpwjc.cn (3P)](http://www.hzpwjc.cn/cir/inci2021/08138.html)
- A second compliance database also gives IECIC serial number 08138 and "authority" (CIR) safe levels of 2% leave-on and 0.3% rinse-off. — [lv-1.cn (3P)](https://www.lv-1.cn/yuanliao/inci/01808138010.html)
- **IECIC rule:** for ingredients with only leave-on data, rinse-off products may refer to the leave-on value. The original reads 「只有驻留类产品最高历史使用量信息的原料，淋洗类产品可参照驻留类产品信息使用」 ("for ingredients with only leave-on maximum historical use data, rinse-off products may use the leave-on information"). — [中贸合规中心 (ZMUNI)](https://www.zmuni.com/zh-hans/news/guan-yu-yi-shi-yong-hua-zhuang-pin-yuan-liao-mu-lu-de-zhei/)
- Media report that the 2021 inventory includes about 18–20 olive-related ingredients, with olive leaf extract the most widely used. — [界面新闻 (Jiemian)](https://www.jiemian.com/article/8424857.html)

### Inferences
- **China versus US figures.** The 32% leave-on historical maximum in China is 16 times the 2% US leave-on maximum reported to CIR. A historical-use figure records past use, not a safety endorsement. It also likely reflects extract solutions of very different strength (for example, a glycerin or water carrier).
- **Possible rinse-off value.** If no rinse-off value is listed, the 32% leave-on figure may serve for rinse-off under the IECIC rule, but this is not confirmed.

### Gaps
- The official NMPA or NIFDC (China's national drug and medical-device testing institute) IECIC 2021 text, including the rinse-off column for 08138, was not retrieved; there is no first-hand verification of the 32% value. Another researcher covers China broadly.

## 6. Skin-irritation data for oleuropein breakdown products (oleuropein aglycone, hydroxytyrosol, elenolic acid) at cosmetic levels

### Takeaway
**Not found.** No published dermal irritation, HRIPT or in vitro sensitization data (such as DPRA, KeratinoSens or h-CLAT) were located for oleuropein aglycone, elenolic acid or hydroxytyrosol at cosmetic levels. CIR did not evaluate these constituents individually; it assessed whole extracts. The only safety context for hydroxytyrosol is oral (EFSA).

### Cited Findings
- CIR treats each olive ingredient "as a whole complex substance rather than judging single components". — [CIR Final Report](https://www.cir-safety.org/sites/default/files/FR_Olive_092023.pdf)
- Searches for HRIPT and in vitro skin-sensitization data (DPRA, KeratinoSens, h-CLAT) on hydroxytyrosol or oleuropein returned only method papers and patents. The patents describe hydroxytyrosol and oleuropein *protecting* keratinocytes from H2O2 and doxorubicin damage, which is cytoprotection, not sensitization data. — [US patent 10231939](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10231939)
- **Supplier page (non-authoritative):** hydroxytyrosol is "generally considered safe for cosmetic use, with minimal reported side effects". It adds that individuals with hypersensitivity to olive-derived products "may experience mild skin irritation or allergic reactions". No data are cited. — [SpecialChem: Hydroxytyrosol (3P)](https://www.specialchem.com/cosmetics/inci-ingredients/hydroxytyrosol)
- **Oral NOAELs for systemic context only:**
  - synthetic hydroxytyrosol: 50 mg/kg bw/day (EFSA 2017, subchronic study); — [EFSA Journal 4728](https://efsa.europa.eu/en/efsajournal/pub/4728)
  - a pure-hydroxytyrosol toxicology paper (Food Chem Toxicol 2013): 500 mg/kg/day was proposed. This value was not verified beyond the search extract. — [search extract, bioseb summary](https://bioseb.com/en/module/prestablog/news?id=475)
- **Human topical oleuropein:** the only human topical exposure data found were the UVB study (Section 3), which reported no irritation endpoint. — [Univ. Pavia repository](https://iris.unipv.it/handle/11571/140790)

### Inferences
- **Indirect evidence on breakdown products.** Commercial leaf extracts naturally contain varying amounts of oleuropein degradation products, and hydroxytyrosol and aglycones form during processing and storage. The negative human irritation and sensitization tests on whole leaf extracts (Section 1) therefore indirectly cover them at the levels present in those test materials. This is plausible but unproven, and how much of these products the tested extracts contained is unknown.

### Gaps
- No dermal irritation or sensitization data were found for oleuropein aglycone, elenolic acid or hydroxytyrosol.
- No CIR or SCCS assessment of hydroxytyrosol or oleuropein as standalone cosmetic ingredients was found.
- No data were found on whether these breakdown products build up in an aqueous gel over its shelf life.
