## 1. Raw-material control at industrial scale: cultivar, leaf age, harvest timing, drying and enzyme inactivation

### Takeaway
Post-harvest handling is the biggest controllable lever, and it is enzyme-driven. Depending on the drying regime, studies report a roughly 6-fold difference in leaf oleuropein (10.0 vs 1.7 mg/g) or losses of up to 93%. Fast, hot drying at 105 °C kept oleuropein above 10% of dry weight. Freeze-drying keeps the leaf's β-glucosidase and esterase active and is not reliably better for oleuropein, although it can enrich oleacein or hydroxytyrosol. Cultivar and season move leaf oleuropein about 6-fold within one region (4.6–27.5 mg/g in Tuscan cultivars), and studies do not agree on a single best harvest month.

### Cited Findings
Drying and enzyme inactivation
- [PR 2026, Univ. Florence, Tuscan cultivars] Oven drying at 105 °C was identified as optimal and gave oleuropein concentrations above 10% of leaf dry weight. Lyophilization, drying at 50 °C, and extraction from fresh leaves caused up to 93% oleuropein loss. — [FLORE record 2158/1473654](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/1473654)
- [PR, Al-Quds Univ.; year not shown] Leaves dried at room temperature gave 10.0 mg oleuropein per g dry leaf; leaves dried at 50 °C gave 1.7 mg/g. Fresh leaves gave the lowest oleuropein, so the authors concluded drying is needed for high recovery. — [Al-Quds repository](https://dspace.alquds.edu/handle/20.500.12213/783)
- [Review, Revista Ceres; year not shown] The review cites oven drying at 105 °C for 3 h as raising oleuropein recovery up to 38-fold over fresh leaves. It also notes that freeze-drying aqueous olive-leaf extracts (not leaves) improves oleuropein recovery. The original study behind the 38-fold figure was not identified. — [SciELO, "Oleuropein: Methods for extraction, purifying and applying"](https://www.scielo.br/j/rceres/a/ZpwXDZP4RkK3Bg3bnfykrvP/)
- [PR 2022, Front. Nutr., Moroccan study] The effect of drying depended on cultivar.
  - Freeze-dried leaves had the highest oleuropein in 'Arbequina' and 'Picholine Marocaine', while 'Koroneiki' and 'Menara' had more when air-dried.
  - Hot-air-dried leaves had significantly more iridoids (oleuropein, secoxyloganin) than fresh leaves. Freeze-drying gave more flavonoid aglycones and hydroxytyrosol.
  - The article states that hot-air drying is the most common industrial method but usually loses bioactives to high temperature, and that freeze-drying is limited by long drying time and high cost.
  - The same record cites a 2012 study in which hot-air drying gave more phenolics, especially oleuropein, than freeze-drying, and another in which hot-air-dried leaves (120 °C) had higher antioxidant properties than freeze-dried ones.
  - Sources: [Frontiers in Nutrition 10.3389/fnut.2022.854680](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fnut.2022.854680/full); [PMC9097227](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9097227)
- [PR 2025, Foods 14(2):313] Freeze-drying "uniquely promotes the selective activation of β-glucosidase and esterase enzymes while simultaneously inhibiting oxidative enzymes" (polyphenol oxidase, peroxidase). Freeze-dried leaves were significantly enriched in oleacein compared with ambient-air and microwave drying, which were also tested. The microwave-drying numbers were not retrieved. — [PMC11764853](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11764853/); [MDPI Foods 14/2/313](https://www.mdpi.com/2304-8158/14/2/313/htm)
- [PR 2022, Plants] Room-temperature, 105 °C and freeze-drying were compared on 'Istrska belica' and 'Leccino', with ethanol-modified SC-CO2, methanol and ultrasound/deep-eutectic extraction. The numbers were not retrieved. — [PMC9003305](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9003305/)
- [PR, Pharmacognosy Research 11(2)] Hot-water blanching of leaves deactivates enzymes. The optimum was 60–65 °C for 15–20 min, followed by ultrasonic extraction. Measured by HPTLC, blanching showed "a significant linear upswing in the concentration of Oleuropein" compared with direct extraction. No absolute values appeared in the extract. — [phcogres PDF](https://www.phcogres.com/article/11/2/178.pdf)
- [PR 2012, Food Chemistry, Stamatopoulos et al.] This study optimized steam blanching as a pretreatment to improve oleuropein extractability. Only the citation was seen, inside a 2021 review of drying, size reduction and blanching; the numbers were not retrieved. — [Processes 2021, 9, 1662 PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/processes/processes-09-01662/article_deploy/processes-09-01662-v3.pdf)

Cultivar, season and leaf age
- [PR, Univ. Florence; year not shown] In Tuscan cultivars, oleuropein ranged from 4,570.0 to 27,547.7 mg/kg dry leaf (about 4.6–27.5 mg/g). It was the predominant compound regardless of cultivar, year or season. Oleuropein and hydroxytyrosol glucoside moved in opposite directions across seasons. — [FLORE 2158/1431674](https://flore.unifi.it/handle/2158/1431674)
- [PR, China, Chemistry & Industry of Forest Products; year not shown] Oleuropein had two annual peaks (February > May) and two troughs (November, April). Leaves from 20-year-old trees had well above the content of 3-year-old trees. Values ran up to 17.2%; the basis (leaf or extract) is unclear in the extract. — [aeeisp lchxygy_1139](https://www.aeeisp.com/lchxygy/article/id/lchxygy_1139)
- [PR, Italy, 15 cultivars of two-year-old plants; year not shown] In every cultivar, oleuropein rose in October and fell in November. — [Univ. Teramo record](https://research.unite.it/handle/11575/100303)
- [PR, Saudi Arabia; year not shown] Oleuropein peaked twice, before flowering and at fruit ripening. The authors found the variation "cannot be explained by temperature". — [PSAU record](https://psau.sa.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/assessment-of-seasonal-variation-of-the-bioactive-oleuropein-in-o)
- [PR 2023, J. HerbMed Pharmacol. 12:505, Iran] Oleuropein was 3.64–18.13 mg/g on an extract basis. It was highest in spring leaves of 'Koroneiki' and 'Zard' and lowest in autumn leaves of 'Mission'. — [JHP PDF](https://www.herbmedpharmacol.com/PDF/jhp-12-505.pdf)
- [Conf 2021, SLACA] In 'Arbosana', 'Koroneiki' and 'Arbequina', harvest period (leaf development stage) "had a strong influence" on oleuropein, at about 11.6–16.5 mg/g dried extract. The best season varied by cultivar. — [SLACA 2021 abstract](https://proceedings.science/slaca/slaca-2021/papers/impact-of-the-development-stage-of-olive-leaves-in-the-oleuropein-amounts-and-antioxidant-activity-of-their-extracts-?lang=pt-br)
- [PR 2024, Natural Product Research] Oleuropein made up 9.8% w/w of the dried leaves studied. Leaf extracts contained 4,900 mg/L oleuropein, compared with 1,800 mg/L in fruit extracts. — [PubMed 38666557](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38666557/)

Fresh leaf versus dry leaf in marketed products
- [PR 2020] Across 10 Australian olive leaf extracts, oleuropein ranged from about 384 to about 11,654 ppm. Products made from fresh leaf tended to have more oleuropein and less hydroxytyrosol than those made from dry leaf. — [PMC7570885](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7570885/)

### Inferences
- The sources agree on a mechanism. Oleuropein is lost through endogenous β-glucosidase and esterase activity when tissue is disrupted, dried slowly, or held at moderate temperatures (50 °C drying; extraction of fresh leaves). Fast high-temperature drying (105 °C) or blanching inactivates the enzymes. Freeze-drying preserves them (Foods 2025), which explains why freeze-dried leaves can lose oleuropein (Florence 2026) or carry more hydroxytyrosol or oleacein (Front. Nutr. 2022, Foods 2025).
- For consumers, a "freeze-dried leaves" claim does not by itself signal more oleuropein. Rapid hot drying, or steam or hot-water blanching before drying, is the mechanistically supported practice. No supplier was found documenting blanching (see Gaps).
- "Fresh leaf" claims face conflicting evidence. Laboratory analyses find fresh leaves give the least oleuropein, while the 2020 market survey found fresh-leaf products tend to give more. The likely deciding factor, which is an inference, is how quickly enzymes are inactivated after the leaf is crushed.
- Variation across cultivar, season and tree age (about 6-fold within Tuscany alone, and 20-year-old trees above 3-year-old trees) means a drug-to-extract ratio such as "10:1" says nothing about potency. Only an HPLC-assayed oleuropein percentage does (see section 5).

### Gaps
- No industrial-scale (tonnage) data were found on how blanching, steam treatment, microwave drying or rapid hot-air drying affect oleuropein. The steam-blanching numbers of Stamatopoulos et al. (2012) and the microwave-drying arm of Foods 2025 were not retrieved.
- The conflict between room-temperature drying (Al-Quds: 10.0 mg/g) and 105 °C drying (Florence 2026: best) is unresolved, because the full texts were not accessible.
- No supplier document was found stating that leaves are blanched or steam-stabilized. Comvita's "within hours" fresh-leaf processing is the only handling claim found (section 7).

## 2. Extraction routes: ethanol–water, PLE/subcritical water, ultrasound, microwave, PEF, ohmic heating, enzyme-assisted, DES/NADES, supercritical CO2

### Takeaway
"Intensified" extraction methods mostly raise yield modestly (about 20–35% relative to the same solvent without the technology) or cut extraction time from hours to minutes. Their crude extracts are not purer: oleuropein was about 2.6% of extract mass for the best ohmic-heating extract and about 8% for pressurized ethanol. That is below the 18–26% of established single-step hydroalcoholic dry extracts. The highest leaf-basis yield, 88.87 mg/g dry matter, used a choline chloride–ethylene glycol deep eutectic solvent, which is not a food- or cosmetic-grade solvent. Pure supercritical CO2 extracts oleuropein poorly.

Scale evidence is thin outside conventional extraction.
- Conventional hydroalcoholic extraction is the only route clearly at industrial scale.
- Subcritical water has one 20 L / 3 kg pilot.
- One commercial NADES cosmetic line included olive leaf (Naturex Eutectys, launched 2015–2016).
- Ultrasound has an equipment vendor marketing industrial units.
- Everything else is laboratory work.

### Cited Findings
Pressurized liquids and subcritical water
- [Univ. Burgos; year not shown] Pilot plant: 20 L batch, 3 kg of dry leaves, subcritical water at 181.1 ± 0.6 °C. The main phenolic extracted was oleuropein at about 1.5 mg/g dry leaf; total phenolics were 33 mg GAE/g. The study's focus was mannitol. — [RIUBU 10259/5855](https://riubu.ubu.es/handle/10259/5855)
- [PR 2012, Xynos et al.] Sequential SC-CO2 (modified with 5% ethanol) followed by subcritical water gave an extract yield of 44.1% and oleuropein recovery of 4.6%. The basis of the 4.6% is ambiguous in the extract. — [Xynos et al. PDF](https://mp.watereurope.eu/media/uploads/2024/10/14/xynos.pdf)
- [PR 2019, Chem. Eng. Trans. 74, Jaski et al.] Continuous pressurized ethanol extraction gave 80.5–82.9 mg oleuropein per g extract (about 8% purity), at laboratory scale. — [AIDIC CET 74/258](https://www.aidic.it/cet/19/74/258.pdf)

Supercritical CO2 with co-solvent
- [PR 2011, Sep. Sci. Technol. 46, Şahin, Bilgin & Dramur] Head-to-head comparison of SFE (co-solvents water, ethanol, methanol) with Soxhlet. The outcome was cut off in the extract. — [avesis record](https://avesis.iuc.edu.tr/yayin/cef85a3b-6872-468f-8110-21893c77fd1a/investigation-of-oleuropein-content-in-olive-leaf-extract-obtained-by-supercritical-fluid-extraction-and-soxhlet-methods)
- [PR 2012, same group] Pure supercritical CO2 performed poorly and a polar modifier was needed. CO2 modified with water was better for oleuropein yield than CO2 modified with ethanol. — [avesis record](https://avesis.iuc.edu.tr/yayin/29fd4636-29b0-4c2d-93ae-7b870d9d60e0/study-on-oleuropein-extraction-from-olive-tree-olea-europaea-leaves-by-means-of-sfe-comparison-of-water-and-ethanol-as-co-solvent)
- [PR 2024] The best ethanol-modified SFE conditions were at 90 °C, giving oleuropein of 1.9 mg/g, "among the highest values recorded". The basis was not stated in the extract. — [PMC11202717](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11202717/)

Ultrasound and microwave
- [PR 2018, Cifá et al.] Optimized ultrasound-assisted extraction (ethanol:water 70:30, leaf-to-solvent 1:5, 2 h, 25 °C) gave about 30% more oleuropein than conventional maceration. — [PMC6021698](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6021698/)
- [PR 2011, Rafiee et al.] Microwave-assisted extraction reached 88.298 mg TAE/g total phenolics in 15 min; maceration reached 69.027 mg TAE/g after 24 h. Ethanol was the best solvent and acetone the worst. These figures are total phenolics, not oleuropein. — [JAPS 2011 PDF](https://thejaps.org.pk/manuscript/2011-japs-433.pdf)
- [PR 2020, Dobrinčić et al.] Microwave, ultrasound and high-pressure extraction all gave more total polyphenols than heat-reflux extraction. Time fell from 60 min to 2 min (microwave) and 21 min (ultrasound). — [LAPSE 2020.1283](https://psecommunity.org/wp-content/plugins/wpor/includes/file/2012/LAPSE-2020.1283-1v1.pdf)
- [Vendor, undated] Hielscher markets ultrasonic olive-leaf extraction under the title "Ultrasonic Olive Leaf Extraction for High-Yield Polyphenol Extracts". The page content was not retrieved. — [Hielscher](https://www.hielscher.com/ultrasonic-extraction-of-olive-leaf-extract.htm)

Pulsed and moderate electric fields
- [PR 2025, Foods 14:368, Razola-Díaz et al.] A PEF pretreatment (0.6 kV/cm, 90 Hz, 11 s) before ultrasound extraction raised oleuropein by 21.7%, hydroxytyrosol by 26.8%, total phenolics by 15.6% and DPPH antioxidant activity by 32.3%, compared with no PEF. — [PMC11816887](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11816887/)
- [PR 2021, Beverages 7:45, Pappas et al.] The strongest PEF effect came with 25% aqueous ethanol and 10 µs pulses: total polyphenols rose 31.85% and specific metabolites up to 265.67%. These are not oleuropein-specific figures. — [Beverages PDF](https://mdpi-res.com/d_attachment/beverages/beverages-07-00045/article_deploy/beverages-07-00045-v2.pdf)
- [UPV; year not shown] A moderate electric field (12.5 V/cm) in water gave a maximum of "0.016 mg oleuropein/g olive leaf d.m.", 34.2% above conventional extraction. These units are as given and look implausibly low against other studies, so verify before use. — [UPV record](https://panorama.upv.es/en/ipublic/item/10365351)

Ohmic heating
- [PR 2024, RSC Sustainable Food Technology, Safarzadeh Markhali & Teixeira] With 15 min of processing for every method:
  - Ohmic heating gave a maximum of 26.18 mg oleuropein per g extract (75 °C, 80% ethanol).
  - Conventional heating gave 7.98–14.55 mg/g and room-temperature controls 8.64–10.81 mg/g.
  - Hydroxytyrosol peaked at 1.38 mg/g extract (80% ethanol, 55 °C).
  - Source: [RSC D3FB00252G](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/fb/d3fb00252g)
- [PR 2024, same group] For ohmic-heating extracts, storage at −20 °C kept compounds best and storage at 25 °C was worst, especially for oleuropein. Temperatures above 110 °C were detrimental to oleuropein. — [RSC D4FB00044G](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/articlehtml/2024/fb/d4fb00044g)

Enzyme-assisted extraction
- [Thesis, Univ. Zagreb; year not shown] Cellulase-assisted extraction (2.5% w/v cellulase in citrate buffer) gave more total sugars and total phenolics than extraction without enzyme. Oleuropein-specific yields were not reported in the extract. — [PBF Zagreb repository](https://repozitorij.pbf.unizg.hr/islandora/object/pbf:4731/datastream/PDF/view)
- [PR 2014, Food Chemistry, Vergara-Barberán et al.] An enzyme method (Celluclast 1.5L at 5% v/v, 30% acetonitrile, pH 5.0, 55 °C, 15 min) for olive-leaf protein extraction gave faster extraction and higher recovery with less solvent. It targeted protein, not phenolics. — [Sigma-Aldrich tech-doc citation](https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/NZ/en/tech-docs/paper/542812)
- [PR 2018, Nanjing Forestry Univ.] Notes that cellulase hydrolysis to convert oleuropein to hydroxytyrosol in olive-leaf extract had earlier "only generated low HT yield". In other words, commercial cellulase preparations attack oleuropein. — [NJFU PDF](https://kjc.njfu.edu.cn/uploads/file/20181205/20181205161530_70188.pdf)
- No study reporting an oleuropein-specific yield from pectinase-assisted extraction was found.

Other physical pretreatment
- [PR 2024] Ball-milling-assisted extraction, optimized by response-surface methodology, recovered about 79% of oleuropein and about 43% of hydroxytyrosol. The authors suggest it could extend to industrial production. — [PMC11314388](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11314388/)
- [PR 2021, Agronomy] "Water as a Solvent of Election for Obtaining Oleuropein-Rich Extracts" describes an organic-solvent-free extraction yielding oleuropein "in high amounts". The numbers were not retrieved. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/5efd3c32a31942efa7ee2f6e416fbd73)

Deep eutectic and natural deep eutectic solvents (DES/NADES)
- [PR 2025, Molecules] Choline chloride:ethylene glycol (1:2, 30% moisture) with a "wall-breaking" technique gave 88.87 mg/g dry matter oleuropein, 4.57 mg/g luteolin-7-O-glucoside and 114.31 mg RE/g total flavonoids. It outperformed lactic acid and methanol; ethanol was not a comparator in the extract. — [PMC11902047](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11902047/)
- [PR 2022, Separations 9:255] A choline chloride:acetic acid DES (1:2) mixed with ethanol (80:20 w/w) was the most effective solvent studied for phenolic-rich extracts. — [MDPI Separations 9/9/255](https://www.mdpi.com/2297-8739/9/9/255)
- [PR 2023, Antioxidants] The optimum for phenolic extraction was choline chloride:glycerol (1:5 molar ratio, 30% water) at 80 °C for 2 h. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/1d23d0c68249491395956eb60e603db8)
- [PR 2025, Antioxidants 14:136] NADES "demonstrated superior extraction performance compared to conventional solvents", with the choline–glycerol system the most efficient. — [MDPI Antioxidants 14/2/136](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-3921/14/2/136)

### Inferences
- When a consumer sees "ultrasound", "microwave", "PEF" or "ohmic-heating" extraction, the honest meaning is faster or modestly higher-yield extraction, roughly +20–35% oleuropein per unit leaf. It does not mean a purer or more potent extract. None of these was found documented by a named ingredient supplier, except that an equipment vendor markets ultrasonic units.
- Extract purity is set by leaf quality, solvent selectivity and post-extraction enrichment, not by the energy input. Pressurized ethanol at about 8% and ohmic heating at about 2.6% are both below the 18–26% oleuropein of established single-step 50–70% ethanol dry extracts.
- "Supercritical CO2 olive leaf extract" is a weak claim for oleuropein. Pure CO2 performs poorly; even with modifiers, yields were about 1.9 mg/g, or 4.6% recovery in the sequential SFE-then-subcritical-water scheme. The technology suits lipophilic fractions better.
- A NADES label means the solvent remains in the product by design; the solvent is the cosmetic carrier rather than a residue. The record-high DES yield used ethylene glycol, which would not be acceptable in a food or leave-on product; this is an inference from general toxicology and is not sourced here. For consumers, "NADES olive leaf extract" claims are real but rare: one documented commercial line (Naturex Eutectys, 2015–2016), with current availability unverified.
- Glycerin, propanediol and fructose liquid extracts are standard cosmetic formats. A label such as "7.5% oleuropein in propanediol" is a meaningful, checkable specification. A label such as "20% extract in glycerin" describes extract concentration, not oleuropein content.

### Gaps
- No pilot or industrial data were found for PEF, ohmic heating, microwave, ultrasound, ball milling, enzyme-assisted extraction or DES on olive leaves. Throughput and cost per kg of oleuropein were not found for any route.
- No pectinase-assisted or enzyme-assisted extraction yield specific to oleuropein was found.
- No source confirmed whether Givaudan still sells an olive-leaf Eutectys NADES extract. A search for its INCI ("Olive Leaf Eutectys") returned nothing.
- The full-text numbers for Şahin 2011 (SFE vs Soxhlet), Agronomy 2021 (water), Plants 2022, and the absolute mg/g values of Cifá 2018 were not retrieved.
- A 2021 study reported NADES oleuropein yields in mg/kg dry weight, highest with glucose–fructose–water, but its record could not be pinned to a URL, so it is excluded.

## 3. Purification and enrichment: resins, ion exchange, membranes, liquid–liquid partition, preparative chromatography, molecularly imprinted polymers, crystallization

### Takeaway
The purity tiers are clear:
- Crude extracts: about 2–26% oleuropein.
- Single resin pass: about 47–70%. Results include XAD-7HP (91% adsorption, 97% desorption), LX-83S (above 70% after crystallization) and the established BMKX-4 (47.6%) and boronate (68.3%) resins.
- Counter-current chromatography: about 90% (60% rising to 90% in a 2009 thesis; above 90% in CN114085258A).
- Liquid–liquid partition in a 2024 paper: above 98%, at laboratory scale.

For hydroxytyrosol made from leaves, acid hydrolysis plus ethyl-acetate partition gives about 60% w/w. Ultrafiltration plus nanofiltration gives 68%. Resin, silica and preparative chromatography reach 95%. Only membranes reached semi-pilot scale in the sources, and no source documented crystallization to high purity.

### Cited Findings
Macroporous and adsorbent resins
- [PR 2017, Chem. Eng. Commun., Turkey] Among the resins tested, XAD-7HP performed best, adsorbing about 91% of oleuropein and desorbing about 97% of what it adsorbed. — [avesis record](https://avesis.iuc.edu.tr/yayin/27804b5c-a036-44ac-b4a3-a2ab1709fbc6/selective-adsorption-of-oleuropein-from-olive-olea-europaea-leaf-extract-using-macroporous-resin)
- [PR 2025, Flavour Fragrance Cosmetics, no. 3, Shanghai and Guilin authors] LX-83S macroporous resin separated best, with elution in 50% ethanol. After crystallization the oleuropein mass fraction was above 70%. — [researching.cn](https://m.researching.cn/articles/OJ30e2f1e9646c703c)

Membranes
- [PR 2022, Membranes 12(11):1027] Olive-leaf extracts were run through ultrafiltration and nanofiltration before or after hydrolysis. The best scheme was a 10,000 Da ultrafiltration membrane followed by a 200–300 Da nanofiltration membrane on the hydrolysed extract. - [PR 2021, Separations 8(9):159] Ultrasound-assisted hydrochloric-acid hydrolysis of leaves gave 14.11 ± 0.12 mg hydroxytyrosol per g. NKA-II macroporous resin was suitable for enrichment. The paper cites a route of methanol extraction, macroporous resin, silica gel and preparative chromatography reaching 95% hydroxytyrosol purity. — [MDPI Separations 8/9/159](https://www.mdpi.com/2297-8739/8/9/159)
- [PR 2022, Antioxidants, Univ. Patras] Extraction with 60–80% ethanol gave almost twice the phenolics of pure water, about 45 g per kg dry leaf. Hydrolysis plus ethyl-acetate extraction raised hydroxytyrosol from below 4% to about 60% w/w of dry extract, or 10–15 g per kg dry leaf. - [Patent, US 9,480,639; year not shown] The background states that HPLC-based and supercritical-fluid approaches to hydroxytyrosol-enriched products are too expensive for industrial scale. The patent concerns olive fruit water, not leaves. — [USPTO PDF link](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9480639)

Reference-grade material (not bulk ingredients)
- [Supplier] Analytical standards are catalogued at purity >98.0% (HPLC) by TCI and ≥98.0% (HPLC area %) by Sigma-Aldrich. An EDQM oleuropein reference standard, "for use in laboratory tests only as specifically prescribed in the European Pharmacopoeia", is also sold. — [TCI O0420](https://tcichemicals.com/OP/en/p/O0420); [Sigma spec sheet 12247](https://perfqws.sigmaaldrich.com/US/en/specification-sheet/SIGMA/12247); [Sigma Y0000805 EP CRS](https://www.sigmaaldrich.com/US/en/product/sial/y0000805)

### Inferences
- Bulk grades at 40% (Bonolive) or 80% (Sabinsa) oleuropein are well above what any single extraction step achieves in these sources: the best was about 26% for established ethanol extracts, about 8% for pressurized ethanol and about 2.6% for ohmic heating. Such grades almost certainly involve an enrichment step such as a resin pass, partition or chromatography. Neither supplier discloses its route (section 7).
- Grades of 90% or more need multi-step chromatography or partition, which costs solvent and yield: CN114085258A cites prior yields of about 1%, and the MIP route recovered 24.2 mg per g of crude extract, or 2.4%. These are pharmaceutical or reference-standard purities rather than typical cosmetic or nutraceutical ingredients.
- Organic solvents used in enrichment imply residual-solvent risk that ethanol-only extracts do not have. They include n-butanol, ethyl acetate and methanol (CCC and partition routes, Patras hydrolysis route) and methanol elution (US 6,849,770, section 6). The relevant certificate-of-analysis items are in section 5.
- Membrane routes add no solvent and reached semi-pilot scale (Patras). They are the most scale-ready "clean" enrichment documented here, but only to about 68% hydroxytyrosol.

### Gaps
- No study or patent was found demonstrating crystallization of oleuropein to above 90% purity. The only crystallization mention was LX-83S at above 70%.
- No ion-exchange data specific to olive-leaf oleuropein was found. The ion-exchange findings relate to hydroxytyrosol from olive by-products (US 6,849,770).
- The claimed purities of CN109021042B and EP1582512, and the recovery of the above-98% liquid–liquid route, were not retrieved.
- No pilot or industrial data for resin enrichment of olive-leaf oleuropein (column size, throughput, resin lifetime) were found.

## 4. Conversion: acid or enzymatic hydrolysis to hydroxytyrosol, fermentation, aglycone, oleacein, elenolic acid, and synthetic or biotechnological hydroxytyrosol

### Takeaway
Leaves contain little free hydroxytyrosol, so "hydroxytyrosol from olive leaf" implies a hydrolysis step. Reported leaf-basis yields converge on about 10–15 mg hydroxytyrosol per g dry leaf: 14.11 mg/g by acid hydrolysis, 10–15 g/kg by acid hydrolysis plus partition, and more than 12 mg/g by NADES one-pot. Enzymatic routes reach above 90% conversion with immobilized β-glucosidase, and a two-step cascade reports above 99% conversion with 96% isolated yield. All of these are laboratory scale.

Lactic-acid-bacteria fermentation gives low titres, about 0.13–0.36 g/L. Oleacein can be made from fresh leaves using the leaf's own enzymes, at about 1% of fresh weight according to a patent. Synthetic "nature-identical" hydroxytyrosol is the documented industrial route: Seprox Biotech (EU novel food, 2017) and Wacker HTEssence (launched 2015). No data were retrieved on elenolic acid production or on microbial (engineered-strain) hydroxytyrosol.

### Cited Findings
Acid hydrolysis
- [PR 2021, Separations 8(9):159] Ultrasound-assisted hydrochloric-acid hydrolysis, optimized by response-surface methodology, gave 14.11 ± 0.12 mg hydroxytyrosol per g, followed by NKA-II resin enrichment. — [MDPI Separations 8/9/159](https://www.mdpi.com/2297-8739/8/9/159)
- [PR 2022, Antioxidants, Patras] Hydrochloric-acid hydrolysis plus ethyl-acetate extraction raised hydroxytyrosol from below 4% to about 60% w/w of dry extract, or 10–15 g per kg dry leaf. The authors call the process industry-common and easily scaled. — [PMC9220257](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9220257/)
- [Patent, EP 1 582 512 A1, Cognis IP Management GmbH, Düsseldorf; filed 2004-03-31, published 2005-10-05] "Process for obtaining hydroxytyrosol from olive leaves extracts". Olive-leaf extracts are hydrolysed (acid or base) to a hydroxytyrosol-rich aqueous mixture, the hydroxytyrosol is adsorbed on a microporous resin (Lewatit-type in the examples) while by-products stay in the water, and it is then desorbed with a solvent. — [EPO Global Patent Index](https://data.epo.org/gpi/EP1582512A1); [EPO publication server](https://data.epo.org/publication-server/rest/v1.2/patents/EP1582512NWA1/document.html)

Enzymatic hydrolysis, free and immobilized
- [PR 2022, Environ. Sci. Pollut. Res., Chatzikonstantinou et al.] β-glucosidase immobilized on chitosan-coated magnetic beads was used in a rotating-bed reactor and a stirred-tank reactor. Oleuropein conversion exceeded 90%, with up to a 2.5-fold hydroxytyrosol enrichment of the leaf extract. The biocatalyst kept high hydrolytic stability after 240 h at 37 °C. - [PR 2024, Bioengineered] Lipase immobilized on octyl-silica converted about 30% of an initial 25 mM oleuropein from a commercial olive-leaf extract in 12 days, producing 6.8 mM hydroxytyrosol. — [PMC11382736](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11382736/)
- [PR, CNR/Univ. Catanzaro membrane-bioreactor work; year not shown] A membrane bioreactor with immobilized β-glucosidase converted oleuropein to its aglycone at about 93% substrate conversion. The records note that in earlier systems the aglycone was not fully separated from unconverted substrate. — [CNR record](https://explore.cnr.it/resource/item/131052); [IRIS UNICZ record](https://www.iris.unicz.it/handle/20.500.12317/63771)
- [PR 2018, Nanjing Forestry Univ.] A β-glucosidase-based conversion of oleuropein to hydroxytyrosol in olive-leaf extract. It notes that cellulase routes gave low hydroxytyrosol yield. — [NJFU PDF](https://kjc.njfu.edu.cn/uploads/file/20181205/20181205161530_70188.pdf)

Fermentation with lactic acid bacteria
- [PR 2023, Fermentation] In olive-leaf brine, *L. plantarum* ATCC 14917 gave 126.89 ± 1.59 mg/L hydroxytyrosol and ATCC-BAA 793 gave 85.93 ± 0.70 mg/L. Ultrasound-assisted extraction of the brine raised these to about 362 and 249 mg/L. — [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/980586c226604dae8b563381a65f4226)

Of 14 strains, *Fructilactobacillus sanfranciscensis* B415, *Lactiplantibacillus pentosus* B506 and *L. pentosus* B307 converted oleuropein to hydroxytyrosol best; the *L. plantarum* strains did not. The optimal time was about 3 days, and pH, glucose and strain were the key variables. — [PMC12304046](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12304046/); [Springer 10.1007/s11274-025-04495-4](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s11274-025-04495-4)

Oleacein and the aglycone via the leaf's own enzymes
- [PR 2025, Foods 14(2):313] Freeze-drying activates β-glucosidase and esterase while suppressing oxidative enzymes, giving leaves significantly enriched in oleacein. No yield or purity figure appeared in the extract. — [PMC11764853](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11764853/)
- [Patent, US 12,466,779; year and assignee not shown] "Method for obtaining oleacein and oleomissional type secoiridoids and for producing respective pharmaceutical preparations". Shredding fresh leaf tissue in water at 15–40 °C converts to about 1% of fresh-leaf weight. Cutting without water drops the yield below 0.1%. Hot-water extraction at about 100 °C destroys the enzyme activity needed. — [USPTO PDF link](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/12466779)

Synthetic hydroxytyrosol (commercial)
- [Reg 2017] Commission Implementing Decision (EU) 2017/2373 of 14 December 2017 (OJ L 337, 19.12.2017, pp. 56–59) authorised synthetic hydroxytyrosol as a novel food ingredient under Regulation (EC) No 258/97.
  - The applicant was Seprox Biotech (Fuente Álamo, Murcia, Spain), which filed with Spain's competent authority in June 2014. Spain's initial assessment of 2 March 2015 was followed by objections from other Member States.
  - The authorisation excludes children under 3 and pregnant or lactating women.
  - Annex I describes CAS 10597-60-1 as a pale-yellow viscous liquid with moisture ≤4.0%.
  - EFSA's opinion of 31 January 2017 (EFSA Journal 2017;15(3):4728) found it safe at the proposed uses.
  - The Annex I purity figure was not retrieved.
  - Sources: [legislation.gov.uk 2017/2373](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eudn/2017/2373/introduction); [EUR-Lex](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dec_impl/2017/2373/oj/eng); [OJ text, oliveoiltimes.com](https://www.oliveoiltimes.com/library/2017-2373.pdf)
- [Supplier 2015–2017, Wacker Chemie] HTEssence is a "nature-identical" hydroxytyrosol.
  - Wacker's own feature describes a "multistage synthesis method" that was scaled up and patented. Wacker calls it the first nature-identical hydroxytyrosol "free of unwanted byproducts and with a defined amount of active ingredient".
  - It was introduced for skin and hair care in 2015. Forms are a spray-dried powder on maltodextrin and a liquid; Knowde lists it as "manufactured in a patented process".
  - A company-presented study (SupplySide West 2017) gave 30 healthy volunteers 30 mg per day for 4 weeks in a randomized, placebo-controlled crossover design and reported lower LDL cholesterol. This is company data.
  - Sources: [Wacker feature PDF](https://www.wacker.com/cms/media/en/documents/feature-pdf/hydroxytyrosol.pdf); [Wacker press release 2015](https://www.wacker.com/cms/en-us/press-and-media/press/press-releases/2015/detail-63297.html); [Wacker press release 2017](https://wacker.com/cms/en-ae/press-and-media/press/press-releases/2017/detail-84736.html); [Cosmetics Business](https://www.cosmeticsbusiness.com/wacker-launches-nature-identical-hydroxytyrosol-112254); [Knowde HTEssence](https://knowde.com/stores/wacker-chemie-ag/brands/htessence)

### Inferences
- Theoretical ceiling, computed from the molecular formulas (oleuropein C25H32O13, about 540.5 g/mol; hydroxytyrosol C8H10O3, about 154.2 g/mol; mass ratio about 0.285): a leaf with 50 mg/g oleuropein can give at most about 14 mg/g hydroxytyrosol.
  - The reported 10–15 mg/g leaf yields therefore imply near-complete conversion of leaves rich in oleuropein (roughly 35–50 mg/g).
  - A claim of hydroxytyrosol "from olive leaf" far above about 30 mg/g leaf would be implausible even for 10%-oleuropein leaves.
- Hydroxytyrosol grades of 95% or more are reachable from leaves only through hydrolysis followed by chromatography. Synthetic hydroxytyrosol is commercially established and EU-authorised, so a high-purity "hydroxytyrosol" ingredient may be synthetic. Supplier wording such as "nature-identical" (Wacker) signals synthesis, not olive origin.
- Fermentation and immobilized-enzyme routes are scientifically real but undocumented commercially for olive leaf. A consumer-facing "fermented olive leaf" or "bio-converted" claim should be read as changing the oleuropein-to-hydroxytyrosol ratio, not as raising total phenolics.
- The oleacein patent and Foods 2025 make the same point. The same enzymes that destroy oleuropein in a badly dried leaf can be harnessed to make oleacein, so "high oleacein" and "high oleuropein" are partly mutually exclusive outcomes of leaf handling.

### Gaps
- Elenolic acid production processes, yields and commercial status were not found.
- Microbial or engineered-strain hydroxytyrosol (for example *E. coli* or yeast titres) and its commercial use were not retrieved, because the search budget ran out.
- The synthetic hydroxytyrosol purity specification in Annex I of 2017/2373 was not retrieved. A "99.5%" figure could not be confirmed.
- The assignee and filing dates of US 12,466,779 and US 8,968,811, and the patent number of Wacker's synthesis, were not found.
- No pilot or industrial demonstration was found for any enzymatic or fermentation conversion of olive-leaf oleuropein.

## 5. Standards: European Pharmacopoeia monographs, other compendial and industry standards, and typical certificate-of-analysis items

### Takeaway
- Ph. Eur. Olive leaf (Oleae folium, monograph 1878) requires at least 5% oleuropein in the dried leaf.
- A separate Ph. Eur. Olive leaf dry extract monograph (Oleae folii extractum siccum, 2313) exists, dating to 04/2009 and still in the 9th edition (2017). Its numeric oleuropein minimum could not be retrieved. EFSA describes an extract under that monograph as specified at ≥20% oleuropein, and a Ph. Eur.-grade extract tested at 24.8%.
- The USP Olive Leaf Dry Extract monograph is a label-claim standard: 90–110% of the labelled oleuropein, on a dried basis.
- UNE 84679 is a Spanish HPLC test method for oleuropein in olive-leaf extracts used as cosmetic raw materials (2017 edition). It is not a minimum-content specification.
- Supplier certificates list an HPLC oleuropein assay plus residual ethanol (≤5,000 ppm), heavy metals, microbiology, and sometimes pesticides, aflatoxins and benzo[a]pyrene.

### Inferences
- The meaningful consumer-facing standard markers are "Ph. Eur. olive leaf dry extract" (monograph 2313) or "USP olive leaf dry extract" compliance, together with a stated oleuropein percentage by HPLC.
  - The leaf minimum of 5% (Ph. Eur. 1878) is a useful raw-material benchmark: leaves below 5% would not meet the pharmacopoeial herbal-substance definition.
  - "Meets UNE 84679" says only which HPLC method was used, not how much oleuropein is present.
- The USP monograph is a ±10% label-claim standard, so a USP-compliant product's potency is only as good as its label claim.
- A certificate that lists residual ethanol by GC, heavy metals by ICP-MS and microbiology is a basic quality signal. Grades enriched with other solvents (section 3) should also list residues of those solvents, such as methanol, n-butanol or ethyl acetate. None of the certificates found did so; this inference rests on the solvents named in the patents and papers.

### Gaps
- The numeric oleuropein minimum and the "dried extract" basis in Ph. Eur. monograph 2313 could not be retrieved; EDQM pages were not reachable. A "16.0 per cent" figure was searched for and not confirmed.
- The USP "Olive Leaf" and "Powdered Olive Leaf" monographs (if they exist) and their limits were not retrieved.
- Chinese standards for olive-leaf extract (forestry, light-industry or group standards) were not searched, because the budget ran out.
- The current edition status of UNE 84679 (2008 vs 2017) is unresolved.

### Inferences
- The patent record splits into three families:
  - Food-grade, mild extraction aimed at oleuropein: ≤88 °C ethanol (US 5,714,150), ≥70 °C water/alcohol with grape (Shodoshima), leaf selection (N-Zyme/Döhler).
  - Chromatographic or partition routes to high purity, mainly Chinese: silica gel, counter-current chromatography.
  - Conversion to hydroxytyrosol: Cognis hydrolysis plus resin; the ion-exchange plus XAD route of US 6,849,770 for olive by-products; synthetic hydroxytyrosol from Wacker and Seprox.
- A brand's "patented extraction" claim, such as BioActor's "patented olive leaf extract", cannot be verified without a patent number. None of the patents retrieved could be tied to Bonolive or Sabinsa.

## 7. Which technologies named commercial suppliers document (in their own wording), versus academic-only

### Takeaway
Named suppliers document only a few process facts:
- Hydroalcoholic extraction with HPLC standardization, at 5–40% oleuropein grades (Naturex 18%; distributors and Chinese suppliers).
- High-purity grades with undisclosed routes: Sabinsa 80%, and BioActor Bonolive described as "patented" at >40%.

- Glycerin, propanediol and fructose liquid carriers for cosmetics.

No named ingredient supplier was found documenting PEF, ohmic heating, microwave, enzyme-assisted extraction, membrane fractionation, molecularly imprinted polymers, counter-current chromatography (outside a Chinese patent), lactic-acid fermentation or immobilized-enzyme conversion for olive leaf. Those remain academic or equipment-vendor claims.

#### Implications for consumer-facing claims (inferences)
- Meaningful claims:
  - An HPLC-assayed oleuropein (or hydroxytyrosol) percentage.
  - Conformity to Ph. Eur. 2313 or USP Olive Leaf Dry Extract.
  - Disclosure of an enrichment step when a grade is ≥40%.
  - Rapid stabilization of leaves (hot drying or blanching) or fast fresh-leaf processing, each of which has mechanistic and peer-reviewed support.
  - A certificate covering residual solvents, heavy metals and microbiology.
- Weak or mostly marketing claims:
  - "Ultrasound, microwave, PEF or ohmic extracted": these give yield and speed gains, not purity, and no named ingredient supplier documents them.
  - "Supercritical CO2 olive leaf extract" for oleuropein: poor yields.
  - "Freeze-dried leaves": inconsistent, and possibly negative for oleuropein.
  - "Patented process" with no number.
  - "UNE 84679 compliant": a test method, not a content standard.
- Grades of 90–98% oleuropein are achievable (counter-current chromatography, liquid–liquid partition) but documented only in the laboratory or in patents. Bulk consumer products at 40–80% depend on resin or partition enrichment whose solvents and residues suppliers do not disclose.
- High-purity "hydroxytyrosol" may be synthetic (Seprox, Wacker). From leaves, it requires hydrolysis, and yields are capped at about 0.285 × leaf oleuropein by mass.

### Gaps
- Supplier process wording was not retrieved for Sabinsa (80%), BioActor (Bonolive), Euromed, Indena, Natac, Biosearch, or Chinese 98%-oleuropein sellers. No Chinese manufacturer offering bulk 98% oleuropein was identified; only TCI and Sigma reference reagents at ≥98% were found.
- Genosa's Hytolive (membrane process) and CreAgri (vegetation-water hydroxytyrosol) were not verified; the search was blocked by the exhausted budget.
- The OLEAF4VALUE project (EU) and Natac's olive-leaf industrial processes returned nothing in search.
- The current status of Naturex/Givaudan Eutectys olive leaf was not confirmed after 2016.
- No cost data (€/kg or $/kg) were found for any purification or conversion route. The only cost signals are qualitative: freeze-drying is expensive (Front. Nutr. 2022), HPLC and SFE are "too expensive" industrially (US 9,480,639), prior chromatographic routes had about 1% yield (CN114085258A), and extraction from olives is "challenging and costly" (Wacker trade-press framing).
- All numbers are abstract or extract level. The full texts were not accessible, so units and bases (leaf vs extract, dry vs fresh) should be checked before precise quantitative comparisons in the final report.
